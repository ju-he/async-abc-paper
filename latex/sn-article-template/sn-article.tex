\title[\methodname{} in Propulate]{Asynchronous, Generation-Free ABC: Streaming AMIS Reweighting for Heterogeneous Simulator Workloads on HPC}

\author*[1]{\fnm{First} \sur{Author}}\email{first.author@example.com}

\abstract{Sequential approximate Bayesian computation (ABC-SMC/PMC) is the standard tool for likelihood-free inference, but its generation-staged structure imposes synchronization barriers that waste compute when simulator runtimes vary across parameters, precisely the regime of large heterogeneous high-performance computing (HPC) workloads. We introduce a \emph{generation-free, single-arrival-driven} ABC algorithm that combines smooth-kernel ABC with a streaming limit of Adaptive Multiple Importance Sampling (AMIS): the proposal mixture and importance weights update after \emph{every} evaluated particle rather than at population boundaries. The algorithm is \emph{stateless} (a pure function of the evaluated history), which makes it crash-recoverable and a natural fit for the barrier-free island model of the Propulate optimization engine. We show that the \emph{idealized} streaming estimator inherits AMIS's consistency and central-limit guarantees under the Wilkinson smooth-likelihood framework. For efficient execution we additionally provide an optional sliding-buffer, top-$k$ approximation of the estimator; it lies outside the scope of those guarantees, so rather than claim the asymptotics for it we supply direct empirical evidence, via simulation-based calibration, that it remains well calibrated. Empirically, against a synchronous pyABC baseline matched on the ABC kernel and bandwidth schedule (so the systems comparison turns on synchronization rather than the acceptance rule), the method (i) is well calibrated: simulation-based calibration on a model with analytic posterior yields empirical coverage close to nominal; and (ii) delivers substantial HPC benefits: under a persistent straggler the synchronous baseline's throughput collapses by roughly $3\times$ while the asynchronous method holds essentially flat, and at fixed wall-clock budget the asynchronous method sustains several-fold higher simulation throughput at moderate-to-large worker counts. On a realistic, expensive simulator workload the asynchronous method scales almost linearly to eight nodes, sustaining several-fold higher throughput ($\approx 3\times$ at $384$ workers, widening with scale) than a synchronous baseline that, given the same core budget and a matching population, scales only sub-linearly, at comparable posterior quality.}

\keywords{approximate Bayesian computation, sequential Monte Carlo, adaptive multiple importance sampling, asynchronous algorithms, high-performance computing, likelihood-free inference}

\section{Introduction}\label{sec:introduction}

Approximate Bayesian computation (ABC) is a standard tool for simulator-based inference when the likelihood is unavailable or too expensive to evaluate directly \cite{beaumont2019abc}. Sequential ABC methods improve efficiency by evolving proposal distributions and tolerance thresholds across a sequence of intermediate targets \cite{sisson2007,toni2009abcsmc,beaumont2009adaptive,delmoral2012adaptive}. In practice, however, most ABC-SMC/PMC implementations remain organized around \emph{synchronous} populations: the proposal and tolerance update only after an entire generation has been evaluated. Because simulator runtimes often depend strongly on the sampled parameters, generation barriers leave compute nodes idle waiting for the slowest simulation, a dominant inefficiency on large heterogeneous high-performance computing (HPC) systems.

This paper introduces a generation-free alternative designed for exactly that regime and implemented within the barrier-free island model of the Propulate engine \cite{taubert2023propulate}. Our contributions are:
\begin{enumerate}
\item A \textbf{generation-free, single-arrival-driven ABC algorithm} that combines smooth-kernel ABC \cite{wilkinson2013,fearnhead2012} with a streaming limit of Adaptive Multiple Importance Sampling (AMIS) \cite{cornuet2012amis}. To our knowledge it is the first ABC algorithm whose proposal mixture updates after every evaluated particle rather than at generation/stage boundaries.
\item A \textbf{history-reconstructed particle archive} that makes the algorithm \emph{stateless} (a pure function of the evaluated history) and therefore crash-recoverable.
\item \textbf{Integration into Propulate}, exploiting its barrier-free island model for true asynchronous execution on HPC.
\item \textbf{Asymptotic guarantees for the estimator, with empirical validation of an efficient variant.} We give consistency and a central limit theorem for the \emph{idealized} full-mixture streaming estimator, as a corollary of AMIS theory under the smooth-likelihood framework and with explicit conditions on the proposal sequence and bandwidth schedule (\S\ref{sec:theory}). For efficient execution we additionally provide an optional sliding-buffer, top-$k$ approximation of this estimator; it lies outside the scope of those conditions, so we establish its inference quality \emph{empirically}, by simulation-based calibration and matched-budget posterior error, rather than by appeal to the asymptotics.
\item An \textbf{apples-to-apples empirical evaluation against pyABC} \cite{klinger2018pyabc,schalte2022pyabc} in which pyABC's acceptor is replaced by a probabilistic-rejection acceptor using the \emph{same} ABC kernel and bandwidth schedule. This matched-kernel baseline lets us attribute the throughput and utilization gains to barrier removal, while we separately verify (via SBC and matched-budget posterior error) that the streaming estimator's inference quality is comparable to the synchronous baseline's.
\end{enumerate}

The remainder of the paper summarizes the relevant ABC and importance-sampling background (\S\ref{sec:background}), develops the method (\S\ref{sec:method}) and its theory (\S\ref{sec:theory}), describes the implementation (\S\ref{sec:implementation}), lays out the experimental program (\S\ref{sec:experiments}), and reports results (\S\ref{sec:results}).

\section{Background and Related Work}\label{sec:background}

\subsection{Approximate Bayesian computation}\label{sec:background-abc}
ABC replaces exact likelihood evaluation with simulation and discrepancy-based acceptance. For observed data $y$ and parameter $\theta$, the ABC target at bandwidth $\epsilon$ is
\begin{equation}
\pi_{\epsilon}(\theta \mid y) \propto \pi(\theta)\, L_{\epsilon}(\theta),
\qquad
L_{\epsilon}(\theta) = \mathbb{E}_{\rho \sim p(\rho \mid \theta)}\!\left[K_{\epsilon}(\rho)\right],
\label{eq:abc-target}
\end{equation}
where $\rho$ is the discrepancy between simulated and observed summaries, $\pi$ is the prior, and $K_\epsilon$ is the ABC kernel. Classical ABC uses the hard indicator $K_\epsilon(\rho)=\mathbf{1}[\rho<\epsilon]$; \emph{smooth-kernel} ABC \cite{wilkinson2013,fearnhead2012} replaces this with a continuous kernel (e.g.\ Gaussian $\exp(-\rho^2/2\epsilon^2)$ or Epanechnikov), which removes the discontinuity in the likelihood approximation and admits standard SMC-sampler theory.

\subsection{Sequential ABC algorithms}\label{sec:background-sequential}
The generation-staged family (ABC-SMC \cite{sisson2007,toni2009abcsmc}, ABC-PMC \cite{beaumont2009adaptive}, and adaptive variants \cite{delmoral2012adaptive,drovandi2011,lenormand2013apmc}) advances through intermediate targets using mixture proposals
\begin{equation}
q_r(\theta) = \sum_{j=1}^{N_r} W_j^{(r)}\, K(\theta \mid \theta_j^{(r)})
\label{eq:proposal-mixture}
\end{equation}
built from accepted particles of the previous stage. The defining constraint is the \emph{generation barrier}: the importance weights at stage $r$ are computed against a single proposal $q_{r-1}$, which requires the full stage-$r$ population before $q_r$ can be formed.

\subsection{Adaptive multiple importance sampling}\label{sec:background-amis}
AMIS \cite{cornuet2012amis} generalizes staged importance sampling: at each stage the proposal is adapted from past particles, and crucially \emph{all} past particles are re-weighted against the cumulative proposal mixture $\bar q_n = \tfrac1n\sum_{j=1}^n q_{\tau_j}$ (the balance heuristic of \cite{veach1995}) rather than against the proposal under which each was originally drawn. AMIS is consistent and satisfies a CLT under regularity conditions. Our method takes the \emph{streaming limit} of AMIS: stage size one, single-arrival-driven adaptation.

\subsection{Asynchronous SMC and parallel ABC}\label{sec:background-distributed}
Off-barrier SMC has been studied for state-space models (asynchronous anytime particle methods \cite{paige2014cascade} and parallel resampling \cite{murray2016anytime}), but none targets ABC, and none combines asynchronous execution with AMIS-style cumulative-mixture reweighting. Distributed ABC frameworks such as pyABC \cite{klinger2018pyabc,schalte2022pyabc} and ABCpy \cite{dutta2017abcpy} parallelize \emph{simulation} effectively but retain population-level synchronization: workers stall while in-flight simulations drain at the generation boundary. Our contribution removes the barrier entirely; the trade-off is that standard generational SMC theory no longer applies, which motivates \S\ref{sec:theory}.

\section{Method}\label{sec:method}

\subsection{Design constraints and history-based state}\label{sec:method-constraints}
We design the algorithm to be \emph{stateless}: every algorithmic quantity is a pure function of the evaluated history, with no mutable state carried between calls. Statelessness is a desirable property in its own right, as it makes the method exactly reproducible and crash-recoverable, each candidate being reconstructible from the recorded history alone. For an efficient implementation we leverage the existing Propulate framework, whose propagator exposes a single call \texttt{\_\_call\_\_(inds) -> Individual}: it receives the entire evaluated history and emits the next candidate without persistent archive state or assimilation callbacks, exactly the stateless, history-based interface our design calls for. All algorithmic state is thus reconstructed from history. Define the evaluated history at call $n$ as
\begin{equation}
\mathcal{H}_n = \bigl\{(\theta_i,\rho_i,\epsilon_i,\tau_i,w_i)\bigr\}_{i=1}^{n},
\label{eq:history}
\end{equation}
where $\theta_i$ is the parameter, $\rho_i$ the discrepancy, $\epsilon_i$ the bandwidth active when $\theta_i$ was proposed, $\tau_i$ the proposal-time index, and $w_i$ the stored core importance weight $\pi/q_{\tau_i}$.

\subsection{Tolerance reconstruction and monotonicity}\label{sec:method-tolerance}
Each proposed individual stores the bandwidth active at proposal time. The effective bandwidth reconstructed from history is the running minimum of these stored bandwidths, $\epsilon_{\text{hist}} = \min_{i}\epsilon_i$ (falling back to the initial tolerance on an empty history). The scheduler proposes $\epsilon_{\text{sched}}$, and the bandwidth used is $\epsilon_n = \min(\epsilon_{\text{hist}}, \epsilon_{\text{sched}})$, giving a monotone-decrease guarantee with no mutable propagator state.

\subsection{Archive and smooth-kernel proposal}\label{sec:method-proposal}
The reconstructed archive is the top-$k$ history members by lowest discrepancy, $A_n = \mathrm{Top}_k(\{\theta_i\},\,\text{order by }\rho_i)$. The proposal mixture uses kernel-weighted archive members,
\begin{equation}
q_n(\theta) = \sum_{j\in A_n}\tilde W_j^{(n)}\, K_\Sigma(\theta-\theta_j),
\qquad
\tilde W_j^{(n)} \propto w_j \cdot K_{\epsilon_n}(\rho_j),
\label{eq:steady-state-proposal}
\end{equation}
with $\sum_j \tilde W_j^{(n)}=1$. The perturbation kernel $K_\Sigma$ is Gaussian with covariance $\Sigma_n = s\,\widehat{\mathrm{Cov}}_{\tilde W}(A_n)$ estimated from the kernel-weighted archive ($s$ is the \texttt{perturbation\_scale} hyperparameter), factored once via Cholesky. Smooth kernels remove the prior-vs-archive discontinuity present in hard-threshold ABC-PMC: every evaluated particle contributes continuously through its kernel weight.

\subsection{Streaming AMIS importance weight}\label{sec:method-amis}
A particle $\theta^\star$ drawn from $q_n$ receives an importance weight under the balance heuristic over a sliding ring buffer $\mathcal{S}$ of past proposals (size $S$, sampled every \texttt{amis\_interval} calls):
\begin{equation}
w^\star = \frac{\pi(\theta^\star)}{\bar q_n(\theta^\star)},
\qquad
\bar q_n(\theta) = \frac{1}{1+|\mathcal{S}|}\Bigl[q_n(\theta) + \sum_{s\in\mathcal{S}} q_s(\theta)\Bigr].
\label{eq:amis-weight}
\end{equation}
Setting $|\mathcal{S}|=0$ recovers single-current-proposal weighting; increasing $S$ enriches the balance-heuristic denominator toward the full cumulative mixture, which lowers the variance of the importance weights at a modest additional per-call cost. We use $S=20$ by default, a value that captures most of that variance reduction while keeping each update cheap. A particle's weight, assigned at proposal time, is corrected by the cumulative-mixture denominator at every subsequent use (the retroactive AMIS step). Because each snapshot $q_s$ is the proposal reconstructed from the history truncated at call $s$, the buffer $\mathcal{S}$ is itself a deterministic function of $\mathcal{H}_n$, so the propagator stores nothing between calls. Equation~\eqref{eq:amis-weight} is the proposal-time importance weight; the \emph{posterior} estimator reported in \S\ref{sec:theory} reweights it by the smooth ABC likelihood $K_{\epsilon_n}(\rho)$ \eqref{eq:posterior-estimator}.

\paragraph{Three weight objects.} To avoid conflation, the method uses three distinct weightings. (i) The \emph{adaptation weights} $\tilde W_j^{(n)}$ \eqref{eq:steady-state-proposal} are kernel weights over the top-$k$ archive; they define the next proposal mixture $q_n$ and so drive proposal \emph{adaptation} only. (ii) The \emph{proposal-time importance weight} $w^\star$ \eqref{eq:amis-weight} is the balance-heuristic weight over the bounded sliding buffer $\mathcal{S}$ ($S=20$), assigned online and used only for the effective-sample-size diagnostic. (iii) The \emph{reported posterior} is the retroactive estimator $\widehat\pi_n$ \eqref{eq:posterior-estimator}, in which every evaluated particle is reweighted by $\pi(\theta_i)K_{\epsilon_n}(\rho_i)/\bar q_n(\theta_i)$ with $\bar q_n$ the cumulative proposal mixture over the \emph{full} evaluated history (Appendix~\ref{app:implementation}); all reported posteriors and quality metrics are computed from it. The bounded buffer in (ii) is the online approximation of the cumulative denominator that (iii) evaluates exactly after the run.

\subsection{Update loop}\label{sec:method-algorithm}
Algorithm~\ref{alg:async-abc} assembles the pieces above into the single-arrival update the propagator runs on each call. Every quantity it uses (the effective bandwidth, the archive, the proposal mixture, and the AMIS denominator) is reconstructed from the history $\mathcal{H}_n$ passed in, so the update carries no state of its own between calls; until the history holds at least $k$ members it emits uniform prior draws to seed the archive.
\begin{algorithm}
\caption{History-reconstructed, single-arrival-driven ABC update}\label{alg:async-abc}
\begin{algorithmic}[1]
\Require History $\mathcal{H}_n$, archive size $k$, initial bandwidth $\epsilon_0$, snapshot buffer $\mathcal{S}$
\State Reconstruct $\epsilon_{\text{hist}}$ from stored tolerances; compute $\epsilon_{\text{sched}}$; set $\epsilon_n \gets \min(\epsilon_{\text{hist}},\epsilon_{\text{sched}})$
\If{$|\mathcal{H}_n| < k$} \State emit a uniform prior draw (bootstrap) \Comment{covers $\bar q$ on the whole box}
\Else
  \State select archive $A_n$ (top-$k$ by $\rho$); form $\tilde W^{(n)}$ in log-space via $K_{\epsilon_n}$
  \State build $\Sigma_n$, Cholesky-factor once; sample $\theta^\star$ by perturbing a $\tilde W^{(n)}$-weighted parent
  \State compute $w^\star$ via the AMIS denominator \eqref{eq:amis-weight}; periodically snapshot $q_n$ into $\mathcal{S}$
\EndIf
\State \Return $\theta^\star$ for external simulation and discrepancy evaluation
\end{algorithmic}
\end{algorithm}

Table~\ref{tab:method-comparison} sets the method against classical ABC-SMC property by property. Classical ABC-SMC advances a fixed population through discrete generations behind a synchronization barrier, weights each stage against the single previous proposal, and carries an explicit mutable archive. \section{Theoretical Analysis}\label{sec:theory}
We state consistency and a CLT as a corollary of the AMIS theory \cite{cornuet2012amis} under the smooth-likelihood ABC framework \cite{wilkinson2013}. The streaming regime (stage size one) is a degenerate case of AMIS stages. The estimator is
\begin{equation}
\widehat\pi_n(\theta) = \frac{\sum_{i=1}^n w_i^{\mathrm{bal}}(n)\,\delta_{\theta_i}(\theta)}{\sum_{i=1}^n w_i^{\mathrm{bal}}(n)},
\qquad
w_i^{\mathrm{bal}}(n) = \frac{\pi(\theta_i)\,K_{\epsilon_n}(\rho_i)}{\bar q_n(\theta_i)}.
\label{eq:posterior-estimator}
\end{equation}

\begin{condition}[bounded proposal-density ratio]\label{cond:c1} There exist $c,C>0$ with $c \le q_\tau(\theta)/\pi(\theta) \le C$ for every generated proposal $q_\tau$ and every $\theta\in\mathrm{supp}\,\pi$.\end{condition}
\begin{condition}[kernel regularity]\label{cond:c2} $K_\epsilon$ is non-negative, integrable, peak-normalized ($K_\epsilon(0)=1$, unit \emph{peak}, not unit integral, as is standard for a probabilistic-acceptance ABC kernel), and Lipschitz in $\epsilon$ for fixed $\rho$.\end{condition}
\begin{condition}[bandwidth schedule]\label{cond:c3} $\{\epsilon_n\}$ is monotone non-increasing with $\epsilon_n-\epsilon_{n+1}\to 0$ and $\epsilon_n\downarrow\epsilon_\infty>0$.\end{condition}
\begin{condition}[snapshot approximation]\label{cond:c4} $\sup_\theta\bigl|\bar q_n(\theta)-\tfrac1n\sum_{j=1}^n q_{\tau_j}(\theta)\bigr|\to 0$ as $n\to\infty$.\end{condition}

\begin{theorem}[Consistency]\label{thm:consistency} Under Conditions~\ref{cond:c1}--\ref{cond:c4}, for every continuous $\pi_{\epsilon_\infty}$-integrable $f$, $\int f\,d\widehat\pi_n \xrightarrow{a.s.} \int f\,d\pi_{\epsilon_\infty}$.\end{theorem}
\begin{theorem}[CLT]\label{thm:clt} Under Conditions~\ref{cond:c1}--\ref{cond:c4} and a finite second moment of $w_i^{\mathrm{bal}}f(\theta_i)$, $\sqrt{n}\bigl(\int f\,d\widehat\pi_n-\int f\,d\pi_{\epsilon_\infty}\bigr)\xrightarrow{d}\mathcal{N}(0,\sigma_f^2)$ with $\sigma_f^2$ the AMIS asymptotic variance at the streaming limit.\end{theorem}

\emph{Proof sketch.} The numerator and denominator are sample averages of $w_i^{\mathrm{bal}}(n)f(\theta_i)$ over $\theta_i\sim q_{\tau_i}$. Conditions \ref{cond:c1}--\ref{cond:c2} bound the conditional weight variance; \ref{cond:c3} makes $\pi_{\epsilon_n}\Rightarrow\pi_{\epsilon_\infty}$; \ref{cond:c4} makes the AMIS denominator converge to the true cumulative mixture. The result follows from \cite[Thm.~1--2]{cornuet2012amis} applied to the streaming limit with the smooth ABC likelihood as target \cite{wilkinson2013}. The CLT yields confidence intervals for posterior expectations, which generational ABC-PMC does not provide without extra machinery. Limitations are discussed in \S\ref{sec:limitations}.

\paragraph{Theory versus the efficient implementation.} Theorems~\ref{thm:consistency}--\ref{thm:clt} characterize the \emph{idealized} streaming estimator that reweights against the full cumulative mixture $\tfrac1n\sum_{j\le n} q_{\tau_j}$. The reported posterior evaluates exactly this object retroactively (\S\ref{sec:method-amis}); the approximations we introduce for efficiency both sit on the \emph{online} path, and are optional in the sense that neither is needed to define or to report the estimator. First, the online proposal-time weight is formed over a fixed-size ($S=20$) sliding snapshot buffer rather than the full cumulative mixture; Condition~\ref{cond:c4} makes precise the assumption that this buffer tracks the cumulative denominator, which is credible once the proposal sequence has stabilized but is not guaranteed. Second, Condition~\ref{cond:c1} (a two-sided density-ratio bound relative to the prior) does not hold for a bare top-$k$ local Gaussian proposal across the whole support; it would require a persistent defensive component, and the uniform prior-draw bootstrap phase (Alg.~\ref{alg:async-abc}) supplies only partial coverage. We therefore read \S\ref{sec:theory} as the asymptotic target the method is designed around, and verify the calibration of the estimator as actually implemented directly by simulation-based calibration (\S\ref{sec:results-validity}).

\section{Implementation in Propulate}\label{sec:implementation}
The algorithm is a stateless Propulate propagator: tolerance schedulers are pure functions of history; the archive is the top-$k$ history members by smallest discrepancy under the reconstructed bandwidth; the smooth-kernel mixture weights are formed in log-space for numerical stability. The reported posterior is the retroactive AMIS estimator \eqref{eq:posterior-estimator}, computed off the timed inference path from the saved history (a pure function of it, hence identical whether computed online or after a crash/restart); the bare proposal-time weight \eqref{eq:amis-weight} is retained separately for the effective-sample-size diagnostic. This retroactive step is a one-time pass of cost $\mathcal{O}(nk)$ in the $n$ evaluated particles and archive size $k$, run after the timed budget and therefore excluded from the throughput measurements by design (the systems comparison is over simulation throughput). For cost-bearing simulators it is negligible against the simulation time; for near-instantaneous simulators whose history reaches $\mathcal{O}(10^7)$ particles it is non-trivial but bounded, and is evaluated in fixed-memory chunks (Appendix~\ref{app:implementation}) so it never dominates wall-clock or memory. For an apples-to-apples comparison, pyABC's \texttt{UniformAcceptor} is replaced by a probabilistic-rejection acceptor using the same $K_\epsilon(\rho)$ and bandwidth schedule as the asynchronous side. The acceptor and kernel are thus matched across methods; the synchronous baseline differs in its generation-staged \emph{execution} (the systems variable under test) and, on the inference side, in retaining classical population weighting rather than the streaming AMIS estimator, whose quality we check separately (\S\ref{sec:results-validity}). Distributed execution uses Propulate's barrier-free island model over MPI; for wall-time-limited HPC experiments the synchronous baseline uses fixed populations/generations, which yields more interpretable comparisons than contrasting methods that stop under different $\epsilon$ rules.

\section{Experimental Design}\label{sec:experiments}

\subsection{Research questions}\label{sec:experiments-rqs}
\textbf{(RQ1)} Does the method recover posteriors comparable to established ABC baselines? \textbf{(RQ2)} Does asynchronous execution improve wall-clock efficiency and utilization under runtime heterogeneity? \textbf{(RQ3)} Does it scale on a realistic, expensive simulator, the regime where running a synchronous matched baseline would itself be prohibitively wasteful?

\subsection{Benchmarks, baselines, and metrics}\label{sec:experiments-suite}
We use four benchmarks of increasing realism (Table~\ref{tab:benchmarks}): Gaussian-mean inference (analytic posterior; sanity and calibration), the g-and-k distribution (classical intractable-likelihood benchmark), the stochastic Lotka--Volterra system, and a realistic, expensive external simulator as the heterogeneous-runtime workload. Baselines are rejection ABC (small problems), the matched-kernel synchronous baseline (a probabilistic-rejection pyABC variant, the main comparator), and pyABC as an external reference. Statistical metrics: posterior mean error, (sliced) Wasserstein distance to the truth, and credible-interval coverage via simulation-based calibration (SBC). Computational metrics: simulation throughput, worker utilization / idle fraction, and wall-clock time to a fixed posterior-error target. Convergence curves are placed on a shared time grid via last-observation-carried-forward resampling so asynchronous (fine-grained) and synchronous (per-generation) records compare fairly.

\subsection{HPC studies}\label{sec:experiments-hpc}
Three dedicated studies probe the asynchrony claim: (i) \emph{stochastic runtime heterogeneity} (a log-normal post-evaluation delay with sweep over spread $\sigma$); (ii) \emph{straggler tolerance} (one worker permanently slowed by $1\times,5\times,10\times,20\times$); and (iii) \emph{strong scaling} at fixed wall-clock budget over worker counts from $1$ up to $288$ (six fully-packed nodes) on Lotka--Volterra and up to $384$ (eight nodes) on the realistic workload, with up to five replicates each. A hyperparameter \emph{sensitivity} grid (archive size, perturbation scale, tolerance scheduler, initial tolerance) and an \emph{ablation} (hard vs.\ smooth kernel; with vs.\ without AMIS reweighting) complete the suite.

\section{Results}\label{sec:results}
Unless noted, results are five-replicate medians on JUWELS; figures compare the matched-kernel synchronous baseline (labelled \emph{Synchronous}) against the proposed asynchronous method (\emph{Asynchronous}, ours). We lead with the computational evidence (RQ2), the paper's central claim, then verify that inference quality is not sacrificed (RQ1), and close with the realistic workload (RQ3).

\subsection{Computational advantage (RQ2)}\label{sec:results-hpc}
\paragraph{Straggler robustness.} This serves as a clean demonstration of the barrier-free advantage (Fig.~\ref{fig:straggler}). As a single worker is slowed from $1\times$ to $5\times$, the synchronous baseline's throughput collapses by roughly $3\times$ (median $\approx 965 \to 311$ simulations/s) because each generation blocks on the straggler, whereas the asynchronous method routes work to available workers and holds essentially flat ($\approx 538 \to 520$ simulations/s), at comparable posterior quality (\S\ref{sec:results-validity}).

\paragraph{Runtime heterogeneity.} Under stochastic log-normal runtime noise the asynchronous method sustains higher utilization (lower idle fraction) than the synchronous baseline, which idles at generation barriers (Fig.~\ref{fig:hetero}). Crucially, this utilization converts directly into inference quality: at a fixed wall-clock budget the synchronous baseline completes fewer and fewer simulations as the runtime spread $\sigma$ grows (it idles at every barrier) and its posterior-mean error to the analytic Gaussian posterior climbs by roughly an order of magnitude, whereas the asynchronous method completes several-fold more simulations and holds its error essentially flat (Fig.~\ref{fig:hetero-quality}). Removing the barrier therefore preserves not just the simulation rate but the \emph{inference} delivered per unit wall-clock.

\paragraph{Parameter-coupled runtime (every-particle bias).} The study above slows simulations independently of their parameters; a sharper test couples runtime \emph{to the inferred parameter}, directly probing the every-particle bias of Limitation~(iv). On the Gaussian-mean benchmark ($48$ workers, $60$\,s budget, five replicates) we set the post-evaluation delay to $d(\mu)=\min\!\big(d_0\,e^{\,c(\mu-\mu_c)/\ell},\,d_{\max}\big)$ with base $d_0=0.02$\,s, centre $\mu_c=0$, length scale $\ell=0.15$, and cap $d_{\max}=0.5$\,s, and sweep the coupling strength $c\in\{0,0.5,1,2\}$ ($c=0$ recovers uniform runtime). Larger $c$ makes one region of parameter space evaluate far faster than its mirror image, so the asynchronous ``keep every accepted particle'' rule over-represents the fast region in the raw archive. The coupling has the intended effect: aggregate throughput falls by ${\approx}40\%$ from $c=0$ to the steepest coupling for both methods (Fig.~\ref{fig:param-bias}a), confirming that the fast region dominates the raw sample counts. Yet the asynchronous posterior error (Wasserstein to the analytic Gaussian posterior) does not increase as the coupling steepens; it stays within the ${\approx}0.07$--$0.09$ band and comparable to the discard-latecomers synchronous baseline (${\approx}0.07$; Fig.~\ref{fig:param-bias}b), because the AMIS importance weights, which divide by the cumulative proposal density, empirically cancel the over-representation. The small async--sync offset is already present at zero coupling and does not widen with it, so the bias is measurable in the sampling rate but not detectable in posterior quality at the coupling levels tested; we discuss its uncorrected status in Limitation~(iv).

\paragraph{Strong scaling.} Lotka--Volterra is deliberately an \emph{adversarial} simulator for a scaling study: each evaluation takes only $\approx 2$\,ms, so the simulator occupies only a small fraction of each worker's wall-clock and there is essentially no per-evaluation compute to distribute. A dedicated instrumented run measures this split directly (Fig.~\ref{fig:lv-timing}): at the $48$-worker throughput peak a worker spends just $4.3\%$ of its time inside the simulator and a further $6.1\%$ on per-arrival proposal/AMIS reconstruction, leaving $\approx 90\%$ for coordination and idle waiting; that residual climbs to $96\%$ and then $98\%$ as the job spreads from one node to three to six. The mechanism is the method's single shared population: every completed evaluation is broadcast to all workers so that each can propose from the current archive, an all-to-all exchange whose per-arrival cost grows with the worker count. On a near-instantaneous simulator this exchange saturates: per-rank instrumentation shows that beyond one node only about one node's worth of ranks ($\approx 48$) stay productive while the rest stall on the message stream, \emph{independently} of how many nodes are allocated, so the residual above is dominated by idle waiting rather than useful work. Aggregate throughput therefore rises almost linearly while the job fits on one node ($16\to115\to295\to595$ sims/s at $1\to4\to16\to48$ workers) and then, across fully packed multi-node jobs (worker counts that are exact multiples of $48$), holds near its one-node peak and declines only gently ($595\to597\to563\to546\to529$ sims/s at $48\to144\to192\to240\to288$ workers, i.e.\ $1\to3\to4\to5\to6$ nodes): the productive rank set does not grow, so neither does throughput. This is a property of coordinating a near-instantaneous simulator across a single shared population, not of asynchrony as such (precisely the regime in which asynchrony has least to offer), and it lifts once the simulator carries real per-evaluation cost that amortizes the coordination (the realistic-workload result of \S\ref{sec:results-realistic}; Fig.~\ref{fig:scaling}, right). To keep the comparison fair at high core counts we give the synchronous ABC-SMC baseline a \emph{population size equal to the worker count} (a smaller population cannot occupy every core); it then uses all allocated cores and scales weakly, from $146$ to $259$ sims/s over $48$ to $288$ workers (Table~\ref{tab:scaling}). The asynchronous method still leads at every scale, but on this free simulator its advantage \emph{erodes} with the job size (from ${\approx}4\times$ on one node to ${\approx}2\times$ at $288$ workers) and it does so while leaving most of the allocation idle. At a single worker the asynchronous method is slower than the baseline, as expected when there is no parallelism to amortize the per-arrival overhead.

\subsection{Posterior validity (RQ1)}\label{sec:results-validity}
Having established the systems advantage, we verify it does not come at the cost of inference quality: across calibration and matched-budget posterior recovery, the streaming estimator's inference quality is comparable to that of the matched synchronous baseline.

\paragraph{Calibration.} Table~\ref{tab:sbc} reports SBC empirical coverage on the Gaussian-mean model (100 trials). The asynchronous method tracks the nominal level closely ($0.84/0.86/0.94$ at the $0.80/0.90/0.95$ levels); the synchronous baseline under-covers somewhat ($0.68$ and $0.80$ at the $0.80$ and $0.90$ levels). We do not over-interpret this apparent ordering: with 100 trials a coverage estimate carries a Monte Carlo standard error of ${\approx}\,\pm0.03$--$0.05$, comparable to the gap, and the test here is limited to a one-dimensional model with an analytic posterior, not the higher-dimensional or intractable settings where the archive/proposal approximations are most likely to bite. The defensible reading is that the streaming estimator is \emph{at least as well calibrated} as the matched synchronous baseline on this benchmark, not that either is exactly nominal. Figure~\ref{fig:sbc} shows the corresponding coverage curve and rank histogram.

\subsection{Realistic simulator workload (RQ3)}\label{sec:results-realistic}
The realistic-workload benchmark runs end-to-end with a real, expensive external simulator, a realistic, heterogeneous-runtime workload and the regime the method targets.

\paragraph{Strong scaling.} Because each realistic-workload evaluation carries real cost, the per-arrival coordination that capped Lotka--Volterra is amortized, and the asynchronous method scales almost linearly all the way to eight nodes (Fig.~\ref{fig:scaling}, right): median throughput rises from $0.25$ to $73.3$ simulations/s over $1$ to $384$ workers and, unlike the near-instantaneous Lotka--Volterra case, keeps scaling \emph{across} the single-node$\to$multi-node boundary, each node-doubling roughly doubling throughput ($9.5\to18.9\to37.1\to73.3$ sims/s at $48\to96\to192\to384$ workers), for $\approx 97\%$ parallel efficiency relative to the single-node rate. The synchronous baseline, given a population equal to the worker count so it too can occupy every core, scales only \emph{sub-linearly} over the same range ($5.9\to7.4\to13.9\to24.1$ sims/s at $48\to96\to192\to384$ workers, ${\approx}50\%$ efficiency), so at $384$ workers the asynchronous method sustains ${\approx}3\times$ its throughput, a gap that \emph{widens} with scale. This is the regime the method is built for: an expensive, heterogeneous simulator where the generation barrier, not per-arrival overhead, is the binding cost. Worker utilization makes the mechanism explicit (Fig.~\ref{fig:realistic-util}): the asynchronous method keeps workers ${\approx}98$--$99.7\%$ busy from $48$ to $384$ workers, whereas the synchronous baseline, even with every core filled within a generation, then waits for the slowest simulation at the barrier, so its utilization settles at ${\approx}39\%$ ($61\%\to42\%\to39\%\to38\%$ from $48$ to $384$ workers, i.e.\ ${\approx}60\%$ idle at every multi-node scale). This barrier idle, not per-arrival overhead, is what holds the synchronous throughput to sub-linear scaling while the asynchronous method tracks the ideal. (One of the three $384$-worker replicates ran anomalously slowly under cluster contention; we report medians throughout.)

\paragraph{Posterior quality.} On the inference side the asynchronous method produces a posterior comparable to the synchronous baseline (Fig.~\ref{fig:realistic-posterior}): $\theta_1$ (the well-identified parameter) concentrates near its reference value, while $\theta_2$ is only weakly identified (for \emph{all} methods, the baseline included). This weak identification is a structural property of the inference problem, not of asynchrony: at this scale $\theta_1$ and $\theta_2$ are near-degenerate (both chiefly modulate the same summary statistic that the features resolve), so neither method separates them and both improve only modestly on rejection ABC. The matched comparison therefore isolates the systems advantage as \emph{orthogonal} to identifiability: both methods are limited equally by the problem, and the asynchronous method delivers its barrier-removal advantage without sacrificing posterior quality relative to the baseline. Establishing the same quality with a synchronous matched baseline at the still larger scales the method targets would itself be prohibitively wasteful, precisely the cost the barrier-free design removes.

\subsection{Ablation}\label{sec:results-ablation}
Removing either core ingredient (the smooth kernel or the AMIS cumulative-mixture reweighting) degrades the estimator relative to the full method, isolating the contribution of each (Fig.~\ref{fig:ablation}). On the Gaussian-mean benchmark the full method attains the lowest final Wasserstein-to-truth and the tightest replicate spread ($0.069$, CI $[0.068,0.070]$); dropping AMIS or reverting to the hard-indicator kernel raises it to $0.076$--$0.077$ (Fig.~\ref{fig:ablation}a), each above the full-method reference, while the remaining hyperparameter variants scatter around it. The effect is modest on this easy analytic-posterior target but consistent: isolating AMIS while holding the kernel, perturbation, scheduler, and archive size fixed (Fig.~\ref{fig:ablation}b), the full method tracks below the no-AMIS variant throughout the converged regime ($\approx 0.072$ vs.\ $\approx 0.077$ after $40$\,s).

\subsection{Sensitivity}\label{sec:results-sensitivity}
Across the full hyperparameter grid \emph{on the Gaussian-mean benchmark} (the analytic-posterior sanity model) the asynchronous method's posterior quality is stable, with no region of catastrophic failure (Fig.~\ref{fig:sensitivity}). The Wasserstein distance to the true parameter stays within $\approx0.08$--$0.17$ across the grid (each cell averaged over five replicates and the two smooth kernels); the tolerance scheduler (acceptance-rate, quantile, geometric-decay) has no discernible effect, the archive size $k$ matters only marginally, and quality degrades only mildly toward the largest perturbation scales and initial tolerances. We therefore did not find delicate tuning necessary to reach the quality reported above \emph{on this benchmark}. We did not repeat the full grid on the costlier non-Gaussian benchmarks, so this robustness statement is scoped to the analytic-posterior model and to the qualitative trends observed there (scheduler-insensitive; mild, monotone sensitivity to perturbation scale and initial tolerance) rather than asserted as a general guarantee.

\section{Discussion}\label{sec:discussion}
The results support the three claims. Asynchronous execution helps most where it should: under a persistent straggler and under runtime heterogeneity, the generation barrier is the dominant cost and removing it preserves throughput that the synchronous baseline loses. For homogeneous, near-instantaneous simulators on small worker counts the asynchronous overhead does not pay off: the single-worker and very-large-worker Lotka--Volterra points make this honest boundary explicit. On the realistic simulator workload (the cost-bearing regime the design targets) the method scales almost linearly while the synchronous baseline, held at the generation barrier even when given a matching population, scales only sub-linearly, at posterior quality comparable to the baseline.

\section{Limitations}\label{sec:limitations}
We are explicit about what \S\ref{sec:theory} does and does not give. (i) AMIS estimators are consistent but not finite-sample unbiased, since proposals depend on past particles; a local-decision rule in the spirit of \cite{paige2014cascade} that recovers finite-sample unbiasedness for ABC is future work. (ii) The bandwidth floor $\epsilon_\infty>0$ in Condition~\ref{cond:c3} means the theorem characterizes the smooth-ABC posterior at the limit bandwidth, not the exact posterior at $\epsilon=0$. (iii) Condition~\ref{cond:c1} is assumed for the specific archive-evolution rule, not proved. (iv) Keeping every accepted particle (unlike pyABC's discard-latecomers rule) over-represents fast-simulator parameter regions in the raw archive. The parameter-coupled-runtime experiment of \S\ref{sec:results-hpc} (Fig.~\ref{fig:param-bias}) quantifies this directly: steepening the runtime$\to$parameter coupling cuts aggregate throughput by ${\approx}40\%$ (so the fast region demonstrably dominates the raw sample counts), yet the asynchronous posterior error does not increase as the coupling steepens and stays comparable to the synchronous baseline, because the AMIS importance weights (which divide by the cumulative proposal density) empirically cancel the over-representation. We therefore do not correct for it algorithmically; a residual finite-sample effect cannot be ruled out, but it is not detectable in posterior quality at the coupling levels tested. (v) The snapshot buffer is fixed at $S=20$; adaptive sizing is a natural extension. Separately, on a methodological note for reproducibility: the post-run retroactive estimator builds its cumulative-mixture denominator over the full evaluated history; for a near-instantaneous simulator that history reaches many millions of particles, so the denominator is evaluated in bounded memory chunks, a fix that does not change the estimate.

\section{Conclusion}\label{sec:conclusion}
We presented a generation-free, single-arrival-driven ABC algorithm that combines smooth-kernel ABC with streaming AMIS reweighting, is stateless and crash-recoverable, and integrates into Propulate's barrier-free island model. Its \emph{idealized} estimator inherits AMIS's asymptotic consistency and central-limit guarantees under explicit conditions, while an optional sliding-buffer, top-$k$ approximation used for efficient execution, which lies outside those conditions, is validated empirically by simulation-based calibration. Against a synchronous pyABC baseline matched on the ABC kernel and bandwidth schedule, the method is comparable in posterior quality to the baseline while delivering substantial HPC benefits on heterogeneous and straggler-prone workloads, including a realistic, expensive simulator workload. Asynchronous, generation-free ABC is a promising approach for large-scale simulator-based inference on modern HPC systems.

\section{Implementation Details}\label{app:implementation}

\paragraph{Tolerance schedulers.} Every scheduler is a pure function of the evaluated history and is \emph{monotone by construction}: the propagator clips any proposed bandwidth above the history-reconstructed running minimum $\epsilon_{\text{hist}}$ (\S\ref{sec:method-tolerance}), so the effective tolerance can only decrease. Let $m$ denote the extra-individual margin (default $m=k$). The three base rules act on the individuals \emph{within} the current bandwidth, i.e.\ the hard-threshold set $\{i:\rho_i<\epsilon\}$. (This bandwidth-membership count is used \emph{only} to drive the tolerance schedule; it is distinct from the smooth-kernel weight $K_\epsilon(\rho_i)$ that defines the proposal mixture and the reported posterior, for which every evaluated particle contributes continuously. We avoid the word ``accepted'' here precisely because acceptance is probabilistic under a smooth kernel.):
\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{Quantile.} $\epsilon \leftarrow$ the lower-rank (non-interpolated) $p$-th percentile of the accepted losses, default $p=50$; applied only once at least $k+m$ individuals are accepted.
\item \textbf{Geometric decay.} $\epsilon \leftarrow \epsilon_0\,\gamma^{\,e}$ after each completed epoch $e$ of $k+m$ accepted individuals, default $\gamma=0.9$.
\item \textbf{Acceptance rate.} Over a sliding window of the most recent $k+m$ individuals, if the acceptance rate exceeds $r_{\text{hi}}$ (default $0.3$) tighten by a factor $0.9$, otherwise hold; the rule never expands $\epsilon$ (the low-rate guard $r_{\text{lo}}=0.1$ simply holds).
\end{itemize}
With a smooth (Gaussian or Epanechnikov) kernel the schedulers run in a \emph{kernel-aware} mode: instead of the hard $\rho<\epsilon$ count, $\epsilon$ is chosen by bisection so that the per-step kernel-weighted effective-sample-size retention ratio meets a target (default $0.95$), which keeps the bandwidth schedule comparable across kernel choices.

\paragraph{Proposal covariance.} The archive $A_n$ is the top-$k$ history members by smallest discrepancy. Component weights are formed in log-space as $\tilde W_j \propto w_j\,K_{\epsilon_n}(\rho_j)$ and normalised (a uniform fallback is used if all smooth weights underflow). The perturbation covariance is $\Sigma_n = s\,[\,\widehat{\mathrm{Cov}}_{\tilde W}(A_n) + \lambda I\,]$ with a fixed jitter $\lambda=10^{-6}$ for positive-definiteness and $s$ the \texttt{perturbation\_scale}; it is Cholesky-factored once per call. Each mixture component is truncated to the prior box, with its log normalising mass computed analytically so the proposal integrates to one on the support.

\paragraph{Matched pyABC acceptor.} For the synchronous baseline, pyABC's \texttt{UniformAcceptor} is replaced, for smooth kernels, by a probabilistic-rejection acceptor that accepts a candidate of discrepancy $\rho$ with probability $K_{\epsilon}(\rho)$ (peak-normalised so $K_{\epsilon}(0)=1$), using the \emph{same} kernel $K_{\epsilon}$ as the asynchronous side; the hard kernel falls back to \texttt{UniformAcceptor}. The rejection step of each replicate draws from an independently seeded generator. This is the sense in which the kernel and acceptor are matched across methods (\S\ref{sec:implementation}).

\paragraph{Bounded-memory posterior estimator.} The retroactive AMIS estimator \eqref{eq:posterior-estimator} evaluates the cumulative proposal-mixture denominator $\bar q_n$ over the full evaluated history. For near-instantaneous simulators that history reaches $\mathcal{O}(10^7)$ particles, so the denominator is accumulated in chunks of $2^{16}=65{,}536$ history points: peak memory is $\mathcal{O}(\text{chunk}\cdot k)$ rather than $\mathcal{O}(n\,k)$, and because the column-wise log-sum-exp is associative across chunk boundaries the result is bit-identical to the unchunked computation.

\section{Additional Experimental Protocol}\label{app:protocol}

\paragraph{Seeding.} Each experiment derives its replicate seeds deterministically from a single base seed via \texttt{make\_seeds}: a \texttt{random.Random(base)} stream emits unique non-negative 31-bit integers, one per replicate, portable across platforms and independent of any global RNG state. Derived seeds (observed-data generation, the pyABC rejection step) use a structured BLAKE2b hash (\texttt{stable\_seed}) of their inputs, so identical inputs reproduce identical seeds across fresh and resumed (\texttt{--extend}) runs.

\paragraph{Cluster and wall-clock control.} Experiments run on JUWELS (Forschungszentrum J\"ulich) under ParaStation MPI, 48 cores per node, scheduled with SLURM. All methods stop at a fixed wall-clock budget enforced uniformly by a monotonic-clock deadline with first-rank-hit semantics: once any rank trips the deadline it serialises its current state into the records and returns, so a partial final generation is recorded rather than discarded. Posterior estimation and plotting run off the timed inference path, so they do not count against the budget.
