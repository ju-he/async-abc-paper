\title[\methodname{} in Propulate]{Asynchronous, Generation-Free ABC: Streaming AMIS Reweighting for Heterogeneous Simulator Workloads on HPC}

\author*[1]{\fnm{First} \sur{Author}}\email{first.author@example.com}
\author[1]{\fnm{Second} \sur{Author}}\email{second.author@example.com}

\affil*[1]{\orgdiv{Department}, \orgname{Organization}, \orgaddress{\city{City}, \country{Country}}}

\abstract{Sequential approximate Bayesian computation advances in generations, and a generation cannot end before its slowest simulation. On a parallel machine that barrier idles every worker that finished early, and its cost is the straggler factor of the workload: the expected maximum of $W$ simulation times over their mean. We introduce a generation-free ABC sampler that updates its proposal after every arriving evaluation, weights every evaluated particle by a streaming form of adaptive multiple importance sampling, and reports an estimator that is a pure function of the evaluated history --- recoverable by replay after a crash, and implemented in the barrier-free island model of Propulate. We prove consistency of the reported estimator, verify its calibration by simulation-based calibration, and measure four things. (i) The barrier costs what the straggler factor says: predicted from the asynchronous run's timing alone, the cost matches a barrierized twin of our own sampler to within $1\%$ under an injected straggler and to within a few per cent on a Cellular Potts tissue simulator at two sizes, across a range from $1.2\times$ to $400\times$. (ii) Removing it converts into a tighter tolerance: on the tissue simulator the asynchronous sampler completes $2.4\times$ the simulations of a matched synchronous baseline, is $1.5\times$ more efficient per simulation, and reaches a $4.1\times$ tighter tolerance at equal wall clock --- close to $40\times$ tighter than a fairly budgeted rejection sampler. (iii) The advantage turns on at a few milliseconds per simulation; below that, per-arrival coordination dominates. (iv) The reported posterior recovers a two-parameter tissue posterior with the truth covered in every replicate, provided the starting bandwidth is set from the prior-predictive discrepancy scale; its effective sample size is bounded by a few multiples of the archive size, which is the method's remaining limit.}

\keywords{approximate Bayesian computation, sequential Monte Carlo, adaptive multiple importance sampling, asynchronous algorithms, high-performance computing, likelihood-free inference}

\section{Introduction}\label{sec:introduction}

Approximate Bayesian computation (ABC) is the standard route to Bayesian inference when a simulator can be run but its likelihood cannot be evaluated \cite{beaumont2019abc}. Its sequential forms --- ABC-SMC and ABC-PMC \cite{sisson2007,toni2009abcsmc,beaumont2009adaptive,delmoral2012adaptive} --- move a population of particles through a sequence of shrinking tolerances, and every implementation we know of does so in generations: the proposal and the tolerance are updated once the whole population has been simulated. That is a synchronization barrier. On a parallel machine with $W$ workers, a generation ends when its slowest simulation does, and every worker that finished earlier waits. The cost is easy to state: if simulation times $T_i$ are drawn from a distribution with mean $\mu$, a generation of $W$ takes about $\mathbb{E}[\max_{i\le W}T_i]$, so the barrier costs a factor $\mathbb{E}[\max_{i\le W}T_i]/\mu$ --- the \emph{straggler factor} of the workload. It is $1$ for a homogeneous simulator and grows with $W$ and with the spread of the runtime distribution. Simulators that are worth running on such machines are rarely homogeneous: the cost of a cell-based tissue simulation, for instance, tracks the number of cells it produces, which varies by orders of magnitude across a prior, and we measure its runtime coefficient of variation rising from $0.05$ to $0.26$ as the simulated volume grows.

This paper removes the generation. We propose an ABC sampler in which the proposal mixture is rebuilt after every arriving evaluation, from a sliding archive of the $k$ best particles evaluated so far; every evaluated particle is weighted against the mixture of proposals actually used --- the streaming limit of adaptive multiple importance sampling (AMIS) \cite{cornuet2012amis} --- and the estimator the method reports is a pure function of the evaluated history. Nothing has to be synchronized between workers, so the sampler runs on the barrier-free island model of the Propulate optimization engine \cite{taubert2023propulate}, and a crashed run is recovered by replaying its log rather than by checkpointing sampler state. Look-ahead scheduling \cite{alamoudi2024lookahead} attacks the same idle time by starting the next generation early on free workers; it keeps the generation, and recovers the $10$--$50\%$ that the boundary wastes. We remove the boundary, and recover the factor.

Our contributions are:
\begin{enumerate}
\item \textbf{A generation-free, single-arrival-driven ABC sampler} combining smooth-kernel ABC with streaming AMIS reweighting, whose reported estimator is history-reconstructed and therefore replayable, with its implementation in Propulate (\S\ref{sec:method}, \S\ref{sec:implementation}).
\item \textbf{A predictive account of what the barrier costs.} We build a barrierized twin of our own sampler --- identical in everything but a collective barrier before each proposal --- and show that its slowdown is predicted from the asynchronous run's timing alone, to within $1\%$ under an injected straggler and a few per cent on a real tissue simulator at two sizes, over $1.2\times$ to $400\times$ (\S\ref{sec:results-barrier}). The same prediction lets a production run estimate its own gain from timing data, without a wasteful reference run.
\item \textbf{The measurement that the gain converts, and where it turns on.} Against a pyABC baseline matched on kernel and bandwidth schedule, the tolerance reached per simulation is at least the baseline's on every benchmark but the one-dimensional analytic one, so throughput multiplies through: $2.4\times$ the simulations, $1.5\times$ per simulation, $4.1\times$ tighter at equal wall clock on the tissue simulator (\S\ref{sec:results-converts}). Both ratios rise monotonically with the cost of a simulation and cross one at a few milliseconds; below that the per-arrival coordination costs more than the barrier (\S\ref{sec:results-boundary}).
\item \textbf{Consistency and calibration of the reported estimator, and its limits.} We prove consistency for the estimator the experiments report (\S\ref{sec:theory}; the proofs and a central limit theorem for damped-adaptation variants are in the supplement), verify calibration by simulation-based calibration in one and four dimensions and under multimodality, and recover a two-parameter Cellular Potts posterior with the truth covered in every replicate. Two settings govern what the estimator delivers: the starting bandwidth, which must be set from the prior-predictive discrepancy scale on an expensive simulator, and the archive size, which bounds the effective sample size at a few multiples of itself (\S\ref{sec:results-posterior}).
\end{enumerate}

Two limits are stated up front. On simulators cheaper than a few milliseconds the method loses to a synchronous one, and two of our four benchmarks lie on that side of the line by design. And the tissue benchmark is a calibration instrument, not a production run: its simulations cost seconds where production runs cost hours, so its role is to establish the method against a known truth and to show that its gain is predictable from timing, which is what a production run with an unknown posterior can use.

The paper is organized as follows. \S\ref{sec:background} gives the ABC and importance-sampling background, \S\ref{sec:method} the method, \S\ref{sec:theory} its theory, \S\ref{sec:implementation} the implementation and the two instruments we compare against, \S\ref{sec:experiments} the benchmarks and metrics, \S\ref{sec:results} the results by claim, and \S\ref{sec:discussion}--\S\ref{sec:conclusion} discussion, limitations and conclusion.

\section{Background and Related Work}\label{sec:background}

\subsection{Approximate Bayesian computation}\label{sec:background-abc}
ABC replaces exact likelihood evaluation with simulation and discrepancy-based acceptance. For observed data $y$ and parameter $\theta$, the ABC target at bandwidth $\epsilon$ is
\begin{equation}
\pi_{\epsilon}(\theta \mid y) \propto \pi(\theta)\, L_{\epsilon}(\theta),
\qquad
L_{\epsilon}(\theta) = \mathbb{E}_{\rho \sim p(\rho \mid \theta)}\!\left[K_{\epsilon}(\rho)\right],
\label{eq:abc-target}
\end{equation}
where $\rho$ is the discrepancy between simulated and observed summaries, $\pi$ is the prior, and $K_\epsilon$ is the ABC kernel. Classical ABC uses the hard indicator $K_\epsilon(\rho)=\mathbf{1}[\rho<\epsilon]$; \emph{smooth-kernel} ABC \cite{wilkinson2013,fearnhead2012} replaces this with a continuous kernel (e.g.\ Gaussian $\exp(-\rho^2/2\epsilon^2)$ or Epanechnikov), which removes the discontinuity in the likelihood approximation and admits standard SMC-sampler theory.

\subsection{Sequential ABC algorithms}\label{sec:background-sequential}
The generation-staged family (ABC-SMC \cite{sisson2007,toni2009abcsmc}, ABC-PMC \cite{beaumont2009adaptive}, and adaptive variants \cite{delmoral2012adaptive,drovandi2011,lenormand2013apmc}) advances through intermediate targets using mixture proposals
\begin{equation}
q_r(\theta) = \sum_{j=1}^{N_r} W_j^{(r)}\, K(\theta \mid \theta_j^{(r)})
\label{eq:proposal-mixture}
\end{equation}
built from accepted particles of the previous stage. The defining constraint is the \emph{generation barrier}: the importance weights at stage $r$ are computed against a single proposal $q_{r-1}$, which requires the full stage-$r$ population before $q_r$ can be formed.

\subsection{Adaptive multiple importance sampling}\label{sec:background-amis}
AMIS \cite{cornuet2012amis} generalizes staged importance sampling: at each stage the proposal is adapted from past particles, and crucially \emph{all} past particles are re-weighted against the cumulative proposal mixture $\tfrac1n\sum_{j=1}^n q_{\tau_j}$ (the balance heuristic of \cite{veach1995}) rather than against the proposal under which each was originally drawn. AMIS is consistent and satisfies a CLT under regularity conditions. Our method takes the \emph{streaming limit} of AMIS: stage size one, single-arrival-driven adaptation.

\subsection{Asynchronous SMC and parallel ABC}\label{sec:background-distributed}
Our proposal-adaptation machinery descends from the adaptive-importance-sampling line --- population Monte Carlo \cite{cappe2004pmc}, AMIS \cite{cornuet2012amis} and its recycling-consistency theory \cite{marin2019consistency}, surveyed in \cite{bugallo2017ais} --- which we take to its streaming, single-arrival limit. Parallel and off-barrier SMC has been studied for state-space models --- the island particle model \cite{verge2015island}, asynchronous anytime particle methods \cite{paige2014cascade}, and parallel resampling \cite{murray2016anytime} --- but none targets ABC, and none combines barrier-free execution with AMIS-style cumulative-mixture reweighting. Distributed ABC frameworks such as pyABC \cite{klinger2018pyabc,schalte2022pyabc}, ABCpy \cite{dutta2017abcpy}, and ELFI \cite{lintusaari2018elfi} parallelize \emph{simulation} effectively within a generation.

The closest work to ours is look-ahead scheduling \cite{alamoudi2024lookahead}, and we want to be precise about what it already does, because it does more than the framing above would suggest. It attacks exactly the idle time we do: rather than let workers stall while generation $t{-}1$ drains, it starts generation-$t$ simulations preemptively on the free workers, from a proposal built either from the preliminary population of the first $N$ acceptances (\textsc{LA Prel}) or from the last finalised population $\mathcal{P}_{t-2}$ (\textsc{LA Past}), and importance-weights each preliminary draw by $\pi(\theta)/g_t(\theta)$ against whichever proposal produced it. It also identifies, and corrects for, the runtime-dependent selection we take up in \S\ref{sec:theory-r}: sampling from the preliminary population biases towards parameters that simulate quickly, which the authors demonstrate on a bimodal model with an artificially slow mode and remove either by proposing from $\mathcal{P}_{t-2}$ instead, or by closing a generation on the $N$ accepted particles that were \emph{started} first rather than the ones that finished first. That start-order rule is the same object our proofs need and our implementation does not have (\S\ref{app:theory-status}).

Two things distinguish our setting. The generation \emph{structure} is retained there and removed here: look-ahead fills the drain window, but a population is still declared, a proposal is still fitted once per population, and the weight is still against a single per-generation proposal $g_t$; we have no populations, adapt after every arrival, and weight against the cumulative mixture of all proposals used so far. And the speed-ups differ in kind accordingly --- theirs are the $10$--$50\%$ recoverable from idle time at the boundary, ours are the one-to-two orders of magnitude available when the boundary itself is what costs (\S\ref{sec:results-hpc}). The two are complementary rather than competing, and we do not claim that established frameworks simply stall at the barrier: one of them already does not. The trade-off we take on is that standard generational SMC theory no longer applies at all, which motivates \S\ref{sec:theory}.

\section{Method}\label{sec:method}

\subsection{Design constraints and history-based state}\label{sec:method-constraints}
We design the algorithm so that the \emph{reported estimator} is a pure function of the evaluated history: the archive, the reconstructed bandwidth and the posterior weights are all recomputed from the recorded log, so a crashed run is recovered by replaying that log rather than by checkpointing sampler state, and the estimator is identical whether it is formed online or afterwards.

That is a property of the estimator, not of the sampler, and the distinction matters. The running propagator does carry state between calls, and it is not merely bookkeeping: the online AMIS snapshot buffer enters the importance denominator of every candidate it proposes, that denominator becomes the candidate's stored weight, and those weights are what the archive's proposal mixture is built from --- so the buffer reaches the proposals, and a restart, which rebuilds it empty, therefore changes the sampler's subsequent trajectory. Two scheduler throttles gate when the bandwidth is allowed to move and do the same. What survives a restart exactly is the estimator over whatever history was in fact evaluated; what does not is the identity of the trajectory (\S\ref{app:protocol}). We are explicit about this because ``stateless'' would otherwise overclaim: the estimator is reconstructible, the run is not replayable. For an efficient implementation we leverage the existing Propulate framework, whose propagator exposes a single call \texttt{\_\_call\_\_(inds) -> Individual}: it receives the entire evaluated history and emits the next candidate without persistent archive state or assimilation callbacks, exactly the history-based interface our design calls for. The archive, the bandwidth and the reported weights are therefore reconstructed from history rather than carried. Define the evaluated history at call $n$ as
\begin{equation}
\mathcal{H}_n = \bigl\{(\theta_i,\rho_i,\epsilon_i,\tau_i,w_i)\bigr\}_{i=1}^{n},
\label{eq:history}
\end{equation}
where $\theta_i$ is the parameter, $\rho_i$ the discrepancy, $\epsilon_i$ the bandwidth active when $\theta_i$ was proposed, $\tau_i$ the proposal-time index, and $w_i$ the stored core importance weight $\pi/q_{\tau_i}$.

\subsection{Tolerance reconstruction and monotonicity}\label{sec:method-tolerance}
Each proposed individual stores the bandwidth active at proposal time. The effective bandwidth reconstructed from history is the running minimum of these stored bandwidths, $\epsilon_{\text{hist}} = \min_{i}\epsilon_i$ (falling back to the initial tolerance on an empty history). The scheduler proposes $\epsilon_{\text{sched}}$, and the bandwidth used is $\epsilon_n = \min(\epsilon_{\text{hist}}, \epsilon_{\text{sched}})$, giving a monotone-decrease guarantee with no mutable propagator state.

\subsection{Archive and smooth-kernel proposal}\label{sec:method-proposal}
The reconstructed archive is the top-$k$ history members by lowest discrepancy, $A_n = \mathrm{Top}_k(\{\theta_i\},\,\text{order by }\rho_i)$. The proposal mixture uses kernel-weighted archive members,
\begin{equation}
q_n(\theta) = \sum_{j\in A_n}\tilde W_j^{(n)}\, K_\Sigma(\theta-\theta_j),
\qquad
\tilde W_j^{(n)} \propto w_j \cdot K_{\epsilon_n}(\rho_j),
\label{eq:steady-state-proposal}
\end{equation}
with $\sum_j \tilde W_j^{(n)}=1$. The perturbation kernel $K_\Sigma$ is Gaussian with covariance $\Sigma_n = s\,\widehat{\mathrm{Cov}}_{\tilde W}(A_n)$ estimated from the kernel-weighted archive ($s$ is the \texttt{perturbation\_scale} hyperparameter), factored once via Cholesky. Smooth kernels remove the prior-vs-archive discontinuity present in hard-threshold ABC-PMC: every evaluated particle contributes continuously through its kernel weight.

\subsection{Streaming AMIS importance weight}\label{sec:method-amis}
A particle $\theta^\star$ drawn from $q_n$ receives an importance weight under the balance heuristic over a sliding ring buffer $\mathcal{S}$ of past proposals (size $S$, sampled every \texttt{amis\_interval} calls):
\begin{equation}
w^\star = \frac{\pi(\theta^\star)}{\bar q^{\mathrm{on}}_n(\theta^\star)},
\qquad
\bar q^{\mathrm{on}}_n(\theta) = \frac{1}{1+|\mathcal{S}|}\Bigl[q_n(\theta) + \sum_{s\in\mathcal{S}} q_s(\theta)\Bigr].
\label{eq:amis-weight}
\end{equation}
Setting $|\mathcal{S}|=0$ recovers single-current-proposal weighting; increasing $S$ enriches the balance-heuristic denominator toward the full cumulative mixture, which lowers the variance of the importance weights at a modest additional per-call cost. We use $S=20$ by default, a value that captures most of that variance reduction while keeping each update cheap. A particle's weight, assigned at proposal time, is corrected by the cumulative-mixture denominator at every subsequent use (the retroactive AMIS step). Because each snapshot $q_s$ is the proposal reconstructed from the history truncated at call $s$, the buffer $\mathcal{S}$ is itself a deterministic function of $\mathcal{H}_n$, so the propagator stores nothing between calls. Equation~\eqref{eq:amis-weight} is the proposal-time importance weight; the \emph{posterior} estimator reported in \S\ref{sec:theory} reweights it by the smooth ABC likelihood $K_{\epsilon_n}(\rho)$ \eqref{eq:posterior-estimator}.

\paragraph{Three weight objects.} To avoid conflation, the method uses three distinct weightings. (i) The \emph{adaptation weights} $\tilde W_j^{(n)}$ \eqref{eq:steady-state-proposal} are kernel weights over the top-$k$ archive; they define the next proposal mixture $q_n$ and so drive proposal \emph{adaptation} only. (ii) The \emph{proposal-time importance weight} $w^\star$ \eqref{eq:amis-weight} is the balance-heuristic weight over the bounded sliding buffer $\mathcal{S}$ ($S=20$), assigned online; it enters proposal adaptation through \eqref{eq:steady-state-proposal} and the effective-sample-size diagnostic, but never the reported posterior. (iii) The \emph{reported posterior} is the retroactive estimator $\widehat\pi_n$ \eqref{eq:posterior-estimator}, in which every evaluated particle is reweighted by $\pi(\theta_i)K_{\epsilon_n}(\rho_i)/\bar q_n(\theta_i)$, where the \emph{retroactive} denominator $\bar q_n$ --- a different object from the online $\bar q^{\mathrm{on}}_n$ of \eqref{eq:amis-weight}, and the one \S\ref{sec:theory} analyses --- is a draw-proportional mixture of $m\le S{+}1=21$ history-reconstructed proposals plus a defensive prior component (Appendix~\ref{app:implementation}) --- a fixed-size stand-in for the mixture the sample was actually drawn from, not the mixture over every evaluated particle; \S\ref{sec:theory} states precisely what that substitution costs. The reported posterior densities and the SBC calibration are computed from it; the Wasserstein-vs-time quality curves are computed on the top-$k$ archive (asynchronous) and the final population (synchronous), matched in size.

\paragraph{Stored fields and weight lifecycle.} Each evaluated individual $i$ records exactly four fields: its parameter $\theta_i$, its discrepancy $\rho_i$, the bandwidth $\epsilon_{\tau_i}$ active when it was proposed, and its proposal-time weight $w^\star_i$ \eqref{eq:amis-weight}; the history $\mathcal{H}_n$ is the arrival-ordered sequence of these records. From $\mathcal{H}_n$ alone: the archive is the top-$k$ members by smallest $\rho$ under the reconstructed running-minimum bandwidth; the adaptation weights $\tilde W^{(n)}_j$ (weight~(i)) are \emph{recomputed} each call from the current archive (never stored) and define $q_n$; the proposal-time weight $w^\star_i$ (weight~(ii)) is computed once at proposal time over the sliding buffer $\mathcal{S}$, stored, and consumed by the ESS diagnostic and by parent selection during adaptation; and the reported posterior weight (weight~(iii)) is \emph{recomputed} retroactively from $\mathcal{H}_n$ using the $m\le S$ snapshot denominator with a defensive prior floor. Only $w^\star_i$ is persisted; weights (i) and (iii) are pure functions of $\mathcal{H}_n$ recomputed on demand, so after a crash the archive and the reported estimator are reconstructed from the recorded history, while the online buffer and the two scheduler throttles rebuild empty and affect only the proposal-time diagnostic.

\subsection{Update loop}\label{sec:method-algorithm}
Algorithm~\ref{alg:async-abc} assembles the pieces above into the single-arrival update the propagator runs on each call. Every quantity it uses (the effective bandwidth, the archive, the proposal mixture, and the AMIS denominator) is reconstructed from the history $\mathcal{H}_n$ passed in, so the update carries no state of its own between calls; until the history holds at least $k$ members it emits uniform prior draws to seed the archive.
\begin{algorithm}
\caption{History-reconstructed, single-arrival-driven ABC update}\label{alg:async-abc}
\begin{algorithmic}[1]
\Require History $\mathcal{H}_n$, archive size $k$, initial bandwidth $\epsilon_0$, snapshot buffer $\mathcal{S}$
\State Reconstruct $\epsilon_{\text{hist}}$ from stored tolerances; compute $\epsilon_{\text{sched}}$; set $\epsilon_n \gets \min(\epsilon_{\text{hist}},\epsilon_{\text{sched}})$
\If{$|\mathcal{H}_n| < k$} \State emit a uniform prior draw (bootstrap) \Comment{covers $\bar q$ on the whole box}
\Else
  \State select archive $A_n$ (top-$k$ by $\rho$); form $\tilde W^{(n)}$ in log-space via $K_{\epsilon_n}$
  \State build $\Sigma_n$, Cholesky-factor once; sample $\theta^\star$ by perturbing a $\tilde W^{(n)}$-weighted parent
  \State compute $w^\star$ via the AMIS denominator \eqref{eq:amis-weight}; periodically snapshot $q_n$ into $\mathcal{S}$
\EndIf
\State \Return $\theta^\star$ for external simulation and discrepancy evaluation
\end{algorithmic}
\end{algorithm}

\section{Theoretical Analysis}\label{sec:theory}
We prove consistency and a central limit theorem for the estimator this paper reports, computed by the algorithm this paper runs. The results are \emph{conditional}, and it is worth being blunt about what that means here: the assumptions below are stated precisely, decomposed, and in part measured, but none of them is \emph{established} for the configuration the experiments use. What the analysis buys is not that the conditions are discharged; it is that the algorithm's departures from the ideal are carried inside the theorem statements as a single quantity, where they can be decomposed and estimated, rather than set beside them as a list of caveats. The proofs are in Appendix~\ref{app:theory}; they are self-contained rather than corollaries of published AMIS theory, whose consistency results for recycling schemes \cite{marin2019consistency} restrict the adaptation so that the weighting stage decouples --- which a single-arrival scheme does not do --- and whose original convergence argument \cite{cornuet2012amis} is heuristic.

The analysis is organized around a single question, because that is all the proofs turn out to need: \emph{is the denominator the estimator divides by the density its sample was actually drawn from?} Write $\tilde q_i$ for the conditional density of the $i$-th candidate given the past, in the sense of the filtration fixed by Assumption~\ref{ass:filtration}. It absorbs every internal departure of the implementation --- the top-$k$ archive rule, the capped in-box rejection, the underflow redraws, the bootstrap phase --- and it is the \emph{only} way the sampler enters Theorem~\ref{thm:consistency}. That such a conditional density exists with respect to a filtration the proofs can use is itself an assumption once execution is asynchronous, which is why Assumption~\ref{ass:filtration} states it rather than leaving it implicit. Sequentially it is exactly the density the propagator emits from; asynchronously the two can differ, and Assumption~\ref{ass:filtration} is where that is confronted rather than hidden. Write
\begin{equation}
\bar q^\star_n:=\tfrac1n\textstyle\sum_{i\le n}\tilde q_i
\label{eq:draw-mixture}
\end{equation}
for the mixture the pooled sample was drawn from, against the reported denominator
\begin{equation}
\bar q_n = w_n\,\pi + (1-w_n)\sum_{s=1}^{m}\alpha_{s,n}\,q_{\tau_s},
\qquad w_n=\max(\nu_n,\delta),\quad \alpha_{s,n}\ge0,\ \textstyle\sum_s\alpha_{s,n}=1,
\label{eq:snapshot-denominator}
\end{equation}
the draw-proportional deterministic mixture \cite{veach1995,owen2000safe} of $m\le S{+}1=21$ history-reconstructed proposals with a prior component whose mass is the observed bootstrap share $\nu_n$ floored at $\delta=0.5/(m{+}1)$, exactly as the implementation builds it (Appendix~\ref{app:implementation}). Throughout, $\epsilon_\infty$ denotes the limit of the bandwidth schedule $\{\epsilon_n\}$, whose existence and positivity are part of Assumption~\ref{ass:stab}(i); $L_\epsilon(\theta)=\mathbb{E}[K_\epsilon(\rho)\mid\theta]$ is the smooth ABC likelihood \eqref{eq:abc-target}, $L^{(2)}_\epsilon(\theta)=\mathbb{E}[K_\epsilon(\rho)^2\mid\theta]$, and the estimator is
\begin{equation}
\widehat\pi_n(f) = \frac{\sum_{i=1}^n W_{i,n}\,f(\theta_i)}{\sum_{i=1}^n W_{i,n}},
\qquad
W_{i,n} = \frac{\pi(\theta_i)\,K_{\epsilon_n}(\rho_i)}{\bar q_n(\theta_i)}.
\label{eq:posterior-estimator}
\end{equation}

\paragraph{What is and is not new here.} The $r$-tilted limit of Theorem~\ref{thm:consistency} is, in itself, the elementary observation that self-normalized importance sampling against a mismatched denominator converges to a correspondingly tilted target; stated for a fixed proposal it would not merit a theorem. Three things are not routine. The scheme is adaptive at every draw, so the argument runs through a martingale array rather than an i.i.d.\ one, and the available AMIS results do not cover it: \cite{cornuet2012amis}'s convergence argument is heuristic, and \cite{marin2019consistency} restricts the adaptation so that the weighting stage decouples, which a single-arrival scheme does not do. The prior floor in the \emph{denominator} bounds every weight by $1/\delta$ with no moment or density-ratio condition, which is what makes the conditional Lindeberg step trivial and lets a local archive proposal --- for which the usual two-sided ratio bound simply fails --- be used at all. And the departure from the ideal is carried as $r$ inside the statements, then decomposed and partly measured, rather than assumed away. We do not claim to weaken the hypotheses of any existing AMIS theorem; we claim a different bargain, in which the implementation's infidelity is a quantity in the conclusion instead of a condition on the algorithm.

\paragraph{Assumptions.}
\begin{assumption}[kernel]\label{ass:kernel} $K_\epsilon:[0,\infty)\to[0,1]$ is measurable with $K_\epsilon(0)=1$ (unit \emph{peak}, not unit integral, as is standard for a probabilistic-acceptance ABC kernel), and for every $a\in(0,\epsilon_0]$ there is $L_K(a)<\infty$ with $|K_\epsilon(\rho)-K_{\epsilon'}(\rho)|\le L_K(a)|\epsilon-\epsilon'|$ for all $\rho\ge0$ and $\epsilon,\epsilon'\in[a,\epsilon_0]$. The limiting target is non-degenerate: $Z_{\epsilon_\infty}=\int_\Theta\pi L_{\epsilon_\infty}>0$.\end{assumption}
\begin{assumption}[denominator class]\label{ass:class} $\Theta\subset\mathbb{R}^d$ is a non-degenerate axis-aligned box and the prior density satisfies $0<\pi_-\le\pi\le\pi_+<\infty$ on it. Each $q_{\tau_s}$ in \eqref{eq:snapshot-denominator} is a positive function on $\Theta$ of truncated-Gaussian-mixture form --- at most $k$ components, means in $\Theta$, weights in the simplex, covariance $\Sigma$ with $\sigma_-^2I\preceq\Sigma\preceq\sigma_+^2I$ for fixed $0<\sigma_-\le\sigma_+<\infty$, and per-component normalizing constants evaluated as the implementation evaluates them. Nothing requires $\bar q_n$ to integrate to one: the proofs use only positivity, boundedness and Lipschitz dependence on the parameters, so an approximate normalizing constant leaves Assumption~\ref{ass:class} intact and shows up instead as a contribution to $r$.\end{assumption}
\begin{assumption}[prior floor in the denominator]\label{ass:defensive} $\delta\in(0,1/2]$ is fixed and $w_n\ge\delta$ in \eqref{eq:snapshot-denominator}.\end{assumption}
\begin{assumption}[adapted filtration]\label{ass:filtration} There is a filtration $(\mathcal{F}_i)$ with $\xi_i=(\theta_i,\rho_i)$ $\mathcal{F}_i$-measurable, $\theta_i\mid\mathcal{F}_{i-1}\sim\tilde q_i$ and $\rho_i\mid\mathcal{F}_{i-1}\vee\sigma(\theta_i)\sim p(\cdot\mid\theta_i)$. Under sequential execution this holds by construction; under asynchronous execution it is a genuine assumption (Appendix~\ref{app:theory}).\end{assumption}
\begin{assumption}[fidelity and stabilization]\label{ass:stab} \emph{(i, consistency)} $\epsilon_n\downarrow\epsilon_\infty>0$ almost surely ($\epsilon_\infty$ may be random), and the denominator tracks the draw mixture up to a limiting ratio: for some $r$ with $0<r_-\le r\le r_+<\infty$,
\begin{equation}
\bigl\|\bar q^\star_n/\bar q_n-r\bigr\|_\infty\longrightarrow0\qquad\text{a.s.}
\label{eq:fidelity}
\end{equation}
\emph{(ii, central limit theorem)} In addition $\epsilon_\infty$ and $r$ are deterministic, a (possibly random) limit $\tilde q_\infty$ of $\tilde q_i$ and $\bar q_\infty$ of $\bar q_n$ exist with $r=\tilde q_\infty/\bar q_\infty$, and, almost surely,
\begin{equation}
\begin{aligned}
&\sqrt n\,\bigl(|\epsilon_n-\epsilon_\infty|+\|\bar q_n-\bar q_\infty\|_\infty\bigr)\to0,\\
&\tfrac{1}{\sqrt n}\textstyle\sum_{i\le n}\bigl(|\epsilon_{i-1}-\epsilon_\infty|+\|\tilde q_i-\tilde q_\infty\|_\infty+\|\bar q_{i-1}-\bar q_\infty\|_\infty\bigr)\to0,
\end{aligned}
\label{eq:rate}
\end{equation}
where $\epsilon_{i-1}$ and $\bar q_{i-1}$ are the bandwidth and the denominator \eqref{eq:snapshot-denominator} formed from the first $i-1$ draws (both known before draw $i$; convention $\bar q_0:=\pi$).
\end{assumption}

Assumption~\ref{ass:kernel} holds for the Gaussian kernel with $L_K(a)=2/(ea)$ and Epanechnikov with $2/a$; it excludes the hard indicator, which is not Lipschitz in $\epsilon$ --- one concrete reason the method uses smooth kernels. The Lipschitz constant is stated per compact sub-interval because it degrades as $\epsilon\downarrow0$; no \emph{deterministic} bandwidth floor is needed, since the proof localizes over $[1/j,\epsilon_0]$ and intersects countably many almost-sure events (Appendix~\ref{app:theory}). Assumptions~\ref{ass:class} and \ref{ass:defensive} constrain only the \emph{reported denominator}, which the implementation builds to that exact recipe. Assumption~\ref{ass:stab} carries everything else. For \emph{consistency}, \eqref{eq:fidelity} is the whole of the sampler's contribution: not that the sampler take any particular form, but that whatever it does be asymptotically reflected in the denominator, up to a ratio $r$ the results carry rather than assume away. The central limit theorem asks more --- the per-draw rate in \eqref{eq:rate} and, through the variance, the limit $\tilde q_\infty$ itself --- so the ``one channel'' reading is exact for Theorem~\ref{thm:consistency} and approximate for Theorem~\ref{thm:clt}. Note the two clauses differ in strength: consistency permits $r$ and $\epsilon_\infty$ to be random, while the central limit theorem needs them deterministic, because the centred integrand must be a fixed function for the martingale arrays to be adapted. That restriction costs nothing in the case of most interest, $r\equiv1$, which is deterministic no matter how run-dependent $\tilde q_\infty$ is.

\begin{theorem}[consistency]\label{thm:consistency}
Let Assumptions~\ref{ass:kernel}--\ref{ass:filtration} and \ref{ass:stab}(i) hold. Then for every bounded measurable $f:\Theta\to\mathbb{R}$,
\begin{equation}
\widehat\pi_n(f)\;\longrightarrow\;\pi^{r}_{\epsilon_\infty}(f):=\frac{\int_\Theta f\,\pi\,L_{\epsilon_\infty}\,r}{\int_\Theta \pi\,L_{\epsilon_\infty}\,r}
\qquad\text{almost surely.}
\label{eq:tilted-target}
\end{equation}
\end{theorem}

\begin{corollary}[exact fidelity]\label{cor:exact}
If $r\equiv1$ --- the denominator is asymptotically the density the sample was drawn from --- then $\widehat\pi_n(f)\to\pi_{\epsilon_\infty}(f)$, the smooth-ABC posterior at the limiting bandwidth.
\end{corollary}

\begin{theorem}[central limit theorem]\label{thm:clt}
Let Assumptions~\ref{ass:kernel}--\ref{ass:filtration} and \ref{ass:stab}(i)--(ii) hold. Then for every bounded measurable $f$,
\begin{equation}
\sqrt{n}\bigl(\widehat\pi_n(f)-\pi^r_{\epsilon_\infty}(f)\bigr)\;\longrightarrow\;\sigma_f\,\mathcal{Z}
\quad\text{stably},\qquad \mathcal{Z}\sim\mathcal{N}(0,1)\ \text{independent of }\mathcal{F}_\infty,
\label{eq:clt-conv}
\end{equation}
where the $\mathcal{F}_\infty$-measurable $\sigma^2_f$ is
\begin{equation}
\sigma_f^2=\frac{1}{Z_r^{2}}\int_\Theta \pi(\theta)^2\,\bigl(f(\theta)-\pi^r_{\epsilon_\infty}(f)\bigr)^2\,L^{(2)}_{\epsilon_\infty}(\theta)\,\frac{\tilde q_\infty(\theta)}{\bar q_\infty(\theta)^2}\,d\theta,
\qquad Z_r=\int_\Theta\pi L_{\epsilon_\infty}r .
\label{eq:clt-variance}
\end{equation}
\end{theorem}

A caveat belongs with this statement rather than after it: the rate conditions \eqref{eq:rate} are measurably violated in the configuration our experiments use (\S\ref{sec:theory-r}), so Theorem~\ref{thm:clt} should be read as covering the damped- or frozen-adaptation variants, not those runs. At $r\equiv1$ the variance collapses to $Z_{\epsilon_\infty}^{-2}\int\pi^2(f-\pi_{\epsilon_\infty}f)^2L^{(2)}_{\epsilon_\infty}/\bar q_\infty$, the familiar self-normalized form. Its structure is readable in either case. Because $L^{(2)}_\epsilon\ge L^2_\epsilon$ by Jensen, \eqref{eq:clt-variance} always exceeds the variance of the corresponding \emph{noise-free} importance sampler that could evaluate $L_{\epsilon_\infty}$ exactly; the gap $L^{(2)}_\epsilon-L^2_\epsilon=\mathrm{Var}(K_\epsilon(\rho)\mid\theta)$ is precisely the price of running one simulation per proposed parameter. For the Gaussian kernel $K_\epsilon(\rho)^2=K_{\epsilon/\sqrt2}(\rho)$, so $L^{(2)}_\epsilon=L_{\epsilon/\sqrt2}$ and that price is computable from the same likelihood at a tightened bandwidth. Convergence is stable rather than merely distributional because $\tilde q_\infty$, and hence $\sigma^2_f$, may be random; it reduces to an ordinary $\mathcal{N}(0,\sigma^2_f)$ limit when the limiting proposal is deterministic. Stability is also what makes the theorem usable, because it survives division by a consistent variance estimate:

\begin{corollary}[per-run confidence intervals]\label{cor:ci}
Under the assumptions of Theorem~\ref{thm:clt}, and provided $\sigma^2_f>0$, with
$\widehat\sigma_n^2 := n\sum_i W_{i,n}^2(f(\theta_i)-\widehat\pi_n(f))^2 / (\sum_i W_{i,n})^2$
we have $\widehat\sigma^2_n\to\sigma^2_f$ a.s.\ and $\sqrt n(\widehat\pi_n(f)-\pi^r_{\epsilon_\infty}(f))/\widehat\sigma_n\Rightarrow\mathcal{N}(0,1)$. The interval $\widehat\pi_n(f)\pm z_{1-\alpha/2}\widehat\sigma_n/\sqrt n$ therefore has asymptotic level $1-\alpha$ and is computable from the stored weights alone, at no extra simulation cost.
\end{corollary}

\begin{proposition}[how far the tilt can move the target]\label{prop:tilt}
Let $A\subseteq\Theta$ and suppose $|r-1|\le\eta_A$ on $A$ and $|r-1|\le1$ off it. Then with $\zeta:=\eta_A+\pi_{\epsilon_\infty}(A^c)$,
\begin{equation}
\zeta<1\quad\Longrightarrow\quad\bigl\|\pi^r_{\epsilon_\infty}-\pi_{\epsilon_\infty}\bigr\|_{\mathrm{TV}}\le\frac{\zeta}{1-\zeta}.
\label{eq:tilt-bound}
\end{equation}
More sharply, $\zeta$ may be replaced by $\zeta_0=\pi_{\epsilon_\infty}(|r-1|)$. Estimating $\zeta_0$ from a stored run requires \emph{untilting}: the normalized reported weights $\widehat w_i$ target $\pi^r$, not $\pi_{\epsilon_\infty}$, so $\sum_i\widehat w_i|r(\theta_i)-1|$ estimates $\pi^r(|r-1|)$; weights proportional to $W_{i,n}/r(\theta_i)$ target $\pi_{\epsilon_\infty}$ and give $\widehat\zeta_0$.
\end{proposition}

\subsection{What $r$ is for this implementation}\label{sec:theory-r}
Everything specific to the code enters through $r$. To attribute it, walk from the density actually sampled from down to the function actually divided by, changing one thing at a time. Let $\bar q^{(1)}=\tfrac1n\sum_{i\le n}q_i$ use the \emph{nominal} archive proposals --- the truncated mixtures the propagator intends, with exact normalizing constants and without the rejection cap or the underflow redraws; let $\bar q^{(2)}$ replace those constants by the implementation's product-of-marginals values; and let $\bar q^{(3)}$ be the $m$-point draw-proportional version of $\bar q^{(2)}$ carrying the observed bootstrap share $\nu_n$. Assume each of the four displayed ratios has a uniform limit, finite and bounded away from zero --- which fidelity alone does not give, since \eqref{eq:fidelity} constrains only their product. Writing $\bar q$ for the reported denominator \eqref{eq:snapshot-denominator},
\[
r=\underbrace{\lim\frac{\bar q^\star}{\bar q^{(1)}}}_{\text{(d) cap, redraws, asynchrony}}\cdot
\underbrace{\lim\frac{\bar q^{(1)}}{\bar q^{(2)}}}_{\text{(c) normalizer}}\cdot
\underbrace{\lim\frac{\bar q^{(2)}}{\bar q^{(3)}}}_{\text{(b) quadrature}}\cdot
\underbrace{\lim\frac{\bar q^{(3)}}{\bar q}}_{\text{(a) floor}} .
\]
The product telescopes for any positive intermediates, so the content is in the choice of intermediates, not the algebra: a different insertion order attributes the total differently, and the factors are a decomposition of one quantity rather than four independent errors. Note also what the first factor carries under asynchronous execution. If completion times steer scheduling, $\tilde q_i$ is the enlarged-field conditional law rather than the density the propagator emitted (Assumption~\ref{ass:filtration}), and that discrepancy sits in $\bar q^\star/\bar q^{(1)}$ alongside the cap and the redraws. The four factors are then not a complete attribution of $r$ to implementation details --- one of them is partly a property of the schedule.

\emph{(a) The prior floor.} When genuine bootstrap draws carry less than a $\delta$ share of the history, $w_n=\delta>\nu_n$ and the denominator holds more prior mass than the sample does. With $u=q_\infty/\pi$ the limiting proposal-to-prior ratio, this contributes
$r_{\mathrm{floor}}=\bigl(\nu_\infty+(1-\nu_\infty)u\bigr)/\bigl(\delta+(1-\delta)u\bigr)$, so $r_{\mathrm{floor}}-1=(\delta-\nu_\infty)(u-1)/(\delta+(1-\delta)u)$: it is exactly $1$ when $\nu_\infty\ge\delta$, below $1$ wherever the proposal is thinner than the prior, and $r_{\mathrm{floor}}\to1$ as $\delta\to0$, i.e.\ as $m$ grows. This is the one contribution available in closed form.

\emph{(b) The snapshot quadrature.} The $m$-point mixture stands in for the $n$-point one, with error at most $V_n/m$ where $V_n=\sum_{i<n}\|q_{i+1}-q_i\|_\infty$ is the total variation of the \emph{reconstructed} proposal path; this controls the third factor above, not the fidelity of the whole. Fixing $m$ therefore trades cost against fidelity; see the remark below.

\emph{(c) The truncation constant.} Each mixture component is normalized by the product of its per-axis in-box masses, which is exact for a diagonal perturbation covariance and increasingly accurate as a component's mass sits away from the faces of the box relative to its own scale, but an approximation otherwise.

\emph{(d) Capped rejection, underflow redraws, and (asynchronously) the filtration gap.} A parent's perturbation is rejection-sampled into the box for at most $992$ attempts, so component $j$ is realized with probability $W_j[1-(1-Z_j)^{992}]$ and the residual is emitted as a prior draw; and a candidate whose proposal-time denominator falls below $10^{-12}$ is redrawn up to five times, the last one being emitted regardless. The cap changes the law relative to unlimited rejection whenever $Z_j<1$, through the residual probability $(1-Z_j)^{992}$; $10^{-3}$ is the scale at which that becomes appreciable rather than a threshold below which it is absent. Under asynchronous execution this factor additionally carries the gap between the emitted proposal density and the conditional law of Assumption~\ref{ass:filtration}, which is a property of the schedule; unlike the other three contributions it is neither measured nor bounded structurally, and enters only through the assumption that this factor has a finite, positive limit.

\paragraph{Measured, in part.} What follows measures two of the four factors, not $r$. Two of the four --- the prior floor and the snapshot quadrature --- can be estimated rather than bounded, because $\widehat\pi_n$ is a post-hoc pass: recomputing \eqref{eq:snapshot-denominator} at a much larger $m$ and at the observed prior share gives a reference against which those factors are evaluated at every particle. The remaining two are \emph{not} captured by such a reference: the truncation constant is bounded rather than measured, and factor (d) is bounded only for its cap-and-redraw part, its asynchronous component resting on the assumed factor limit alone. The figures below are therefore a partial accounting, not a lower bound on the total: the factors are multiplicative and an unmeasured one could in principle offset a measured one. On the Cellular Potts production history ($33{,}748$ asynchronous evaluations across $48$ workers, two correlated dimensions, so that (c) is live), contribution (b) alone gives $\widehat\zeta=0.022$ with $r\in[0.92,1.80]$, and the reported posterior sits $1.1\%$ in total variation from the large-$m$ reference, with posterior means within $0.2\%$ and $1.1\%$ of a standard deviation and the effective sample size essentially unchanged ($0.213$ against $0.228$). On a one-dimensional Gaussian-mean history where the exact $n$-point mixture is computable, (a) and (b) together give $\widehat\zeta=0.042$ and a total-variation distance of $0.26\%$ from the reference that removes (a) and (b), with the reported posterior $0.5\%$ narrower --- the tail-suppressing direction predicted by (a). Of contribution (d), the two events a stored history records --- a rejection fallback and an exhausted redraw --- did not occur once in $12{,}000$ draws; a redraw that succeeded on a later attempt leaves no trace. We do not rule those out: we observe only that triggering one requires a candidate to land where the mixture it was drawn from has density below $10^{-12}$, which is a necessary condition for the event, not a bound on its probability. The bound \eqref{eq:tilt-bound} is honest but loose against these direct measurements, by about a factor of two on the Cellular Potts run ($2.3\%$ against $1.1\%$) and by an order of magnitude on the one-dimensional history ($4.4\%$ against $0.26\%$), as sup-norm-free bounds valid for every $f$ generally are.

\paragraph{Do the reported runs meet the stabilization conditions?} Assumption~\ref{ass:stab}(ii) is the one place where we can do better than assume, because the proposal sequence is reconstructible from a stored history. Rebuilding $q_\tau$ along the Cellular Potts production run and comparing against the terminal proposal separates two rates that behave very differently. The \emph{per-step drift} $\|q_{\tau+1}-q_\tau\|_\infty$ decays as $\tau^{-0.92}$, and the archive's membership changes only $832$ times in $33{,}748$ draws, tracking $k\log n$ at a ratio between $0.64$ and $0.80$ across the whole run --- the rate a record-statistics heuristic predicts, since the $k$-th best of $n$ draws improves with probability ${\approx}k/n$. The proposal path's total variation is therefore $O(\log n)$, and the snapshot clause is comfortably met. The \emph{distance to the limit} is another matter. Fitting $\|q_\tau-q_\infty\|_\infty\sim\tau^{-b}$ gives $b\approx0.33$ over the full range and $b\approx0.14$--$0.23$ when the fit is restricted away from the end, where using the terminal proposal as a stand-in for $q_\infty$ biases $b$ upward. Assumption~\ref{ass:stab}(ii) needs $b>\tfrac12$; at the measured values the aggregate $n^{-1/2}\sum_{i\le n}\|q_i-q_\infty\|_\infty$ \emph{grows} like $n^{0.17}$ to $n^{0.36}$ instead of vanishing.

We therefore do not claim Theorem~\ref{thm:clt} for the configuration these experiments run: its rate condition is not merely unverified there but measurably violated. Theorem~\ref{thm:consistency} is unaffected, needing no rate. Two things temper the negative result and point somewhere specific. The measurement is one replicate of one benchmark over about a decade and a half of $n$, against a stand-in limit, so it is a diagnostic and not an asymptotic statement. The natural explanation --- that the run leaves \texttt{min\_tol} unset, so $\epsilon_n$ was still tightening and the archive was chasing a moving target --- we tested and can reject. Running a two-dimensional Gaussian-mean benchmark serially under the same propagator, once with the data-driven schedule and once with the bandwidth pinned at the tolerance the first run reached a third of the way in (a floor the schedule then sat on for $68\%$ of archive-phase draws), moves $b$ by less than $0.07$ and in opposite directions depending on the fit range: $0.61\to0.67$ restricted, $0.76\to0.69$ over the full range. Both arms comfortably satisfy $b>\tfrac12$. So a tightening bandwidth does not prevent stabilization, and cannot be what breaks the Cellular Potts run.

What does explain it is the target. The Cellular Potts configuration was assembled to exercise a realistic heterogeneous-runtime workload for the systems measurements of \S\ref{sec:results-cpm}: its summaries, discrepancy and parameter ranges were not selected for identifiability, and its two parameters confound. Re-running the serial benchmark with that property deliberately induced --- observing only $\theta_1+\theta_2$, so the target is a ridge rather than a point --- collapses the exponent from $b=0.61$--$0.76$ to $b=0.00$--$0.29$, squarely onto the Cellular Potts range. A confounded target reproduces the failure in a \emph{serial} run, so asynchronous replay is not needed to account for it and the adaptation rule is not what breaks.

The mechanism is transparent once stated: on a ridge the top-$k$ archive slides along the flat direction indefinitely, because there is always a marginally better point further along it, so the proposal never settles and $\|q_\tau-q_\infty\|_\infty$ does not decay at the required rate. Assumption~\ref{ass:stab}(ii) is thus a condition on the \emph{inference problem} as much as on the sampler: it holds comfortably for a well-identified target under the same top-$k$ rule and the same bandwidth schedule, and fails when the summaries do not identify the parameters. We therefore state Theorem~\ref{thm:clt} for the damped- or frozen-adaptation variants when the target is weakly identified, and note that for the identified benchmarks the measured rate satisfies the condition with room to spare.

\paragraph{Fixed versus growing $m$.} Contribution (b) is where the estimator's cost meets its fidelity. With $m$ fixed, the $m$-point mixture tracks the $n$-point one only if the proposal path settles, the error being $V_n/m$. Letting $m=m_n\to\infty$ with $V_n/m_n\to0$ removes \emph{this} contribution to $r$ --- it does not by itself deliver $r\equiv1$, since the other three remain --- and the proofs go through for any $m_n=o(n/\log n)$ \emph{provided the floor $\delta$ is held fixed rather than tied to $m$}. The implementation ties them, $\delta=0.5/(m{+}1)$, so growing $m$ there would send $\delta\to0$, and with it the uniform weight bound $1/\delta$ of Lemma~\ref{lem:bounded} that makes the Lindeberg step trivial and fixes the constants in the law of large numbers; the rate conditions would have to be redone. Decoupling the two is a one-line change to the post-hoc pass and costs nothing (Appendix~\ref{app:theory}, Remark~\ref{rem:growing-m}), at a retroactive pass of $\mathcal{O}(n\,m_n\,k)$ instead of $\mathcal{O}(nk)$; the literal cumulative mixture is the endpoint $m_n=n$ at $\mathcal{O}(n^2k)$. The measurements above locate the practical knee: on the Gaussian-mean history the reported posterior is within $3\times10^{-4}$ in total variation of the exact $n$-point mixture by $m=50$, so a $2.5\times$ more expensive post-hoc pass removes factor (b) almost entirely --- which is not the same as achieving $r\equiv1$, the other three factors being untouched. What the paper reports is the $m=21$ end, and \S\ref{sec:implementation} gives its cost.

\section{Implementation in Propulate}\label{sec:implementation}
The algorithm is a Propulate propagator that carries no archive between calls: tolerance schedulers are pure functions of history; the archive is the top-$k$ history members by smallest discrepancy under the reconstructed bandwidth; the smooth-kernel mixture weights are formed in log-space for numerical stability. The reported posterior is the retroactive AMIS estimator \eqref{eq:posterior-estimator}, computed off the timed inference path from the saved history (a pure function of it, hence identical whether computed online or after a crash/restart); the bare proposal-time weight \eqref{eq:amis-weight} is retained separately for the effective-sample-size diagnostic. This retroactive step is a one-time pass of cost $\mathcal{O}(nk)$ in the $n$ evaluated particles and archive size $k$, run after the timed budget and therefore excluded from the throughput measurements by design (the systems comparison is over simulation throughput). For cost-bearing simulators it is negligible against the simulation time; for near-instantaneous simulators whose history reaches $\mathcal{O}(10^7)$ particles it is non-trivial but bounded ($\mathcal{O}(nk)$ with the fixed $m\le S{+}1=21$ snapshot count), and is evaluated in fixed-memory chunks (Appendix~\ref{app:implementation}) so it stays a bounded fraction of wall-clock and memory. For an apples-to-apples comparison, pyABC's \texttt{UniformAcceptor} is replaced by a probabilistic-rejection acceptor using the same $K_\epsilon(\rho)$ and bandwidth schedule as the asynchronous side. The acceptor and kernel are thus matched across methods; the synchronous baseline differs in its generation-staged \emph{execution} (the systems variable under test) and, on the inference side, in retaining classical population weighting rather than the streaming AMIS estimator, whose quality we check separately (\S\ref{sec:results-validity}). Distributed execution uses Propulate's barrier-free island model over MPI; for wall-time-limited HPC experiments the synchronous baseline uses fixed populations/generations, which yields more interpretable comparisons than contrasting methods that stop under different $\epsilon$ rules.

\section{Experimental Design}\label{sec:experiments}

\subsection{What is tested}\label{sec:experiments-claims}
The results are organised around four claims. \textbf{(C1)} A generation barrier costs the straggler factor of the workload, and that cost is predictable from the asynchronous run's timing alone. \textbf{(C2)} Removing it converts into a tighter tolerance, because the asynchronous sampler is at least as efficient per simulation as the synchronous baseline. \textbf{(C3)} The advantage has a boundary in the cost of a simulation, below which per-arrival coordination costs more than the barrier. \textbf{(C4)} The reported posterior is calibrated, recovers a known truth on a real simulator, and is limited by two settings we identify. Each claim gets one subsection of \S\ref{sec:results}, and each needs a different kind of evidence, which is why the benchmark suite spans four orders of magnitude in the cost of a simulation.

\subsection{Benchmarks}\label{sec:experiments-suite}
Table~\ref{tab:benchmarks} lists the five configurations. The two cheap benchmarks --- a one-dimensional Gaussian mean with an analytic posterior and the four-dimensional g-and-k distribution with a reference posterior --- are where calibration and recovery can be scored exactly, and where the cost of coordinating a near-instant simulator is exposed; the Gaussian mean also hosts the two studies with \emph{injected} runtime heterogeneity, a persistently slow worker and a per-evaluation lognormal runtime multiplier, in which the runtime distribution is known by construction. Lotka--Volterra is a stochastic simulator whose cost varies with the trajectory and whose parameters send $98\%$ of simulations to extinction (Appendix~\ref{app:protocol}); it serves the scaling and boundary measurements and is not scored for posterior quality. The Cellular Potts benchmark runs the real \texttt{cellsInSilico} tissue simulator \cite{berghoff2020cis} end to end. Its inference problem was designed on a screening campaign (Appendix~\ref{app:cpm}): two parameters, the cell division rate and the target cell volume, log-uniform on $[0.001,0.2]$ and $[200,1200]$, that move the two directions the summary statistics resolve --- the cell count and the cluster radius at fixed count --- and a discrepancy over those two statistics averaged over four replicate simulations per evaluation. We run it at two sizes: a $50^3$ box, where one evaluation costs $13$\,s, and an $80^3$ box with twice the duration, where it costs $170$\,s and the runtime coefficient of variation rises from $0.05$ to $0.13$--$0.23$ because runtime tracks cell count ($r=0.99$) and cell count is no longer bounded by the box.

The Cellular Potts benchmark is a \emph{calibration instrument}, and we state its role so that it is not read as a production run. Production simulations of this model take hours on many cores; ours take seconds to minutes, and their inference is on synthetic data with a known truth. What that buys is the ability to say whether the method recovers a posterior it should recover, and to measure the barrier's cost on a real workload at two levels of heterogeneity; what a production project takes from it is the predictor of \S\ref{sec:experiments-metrics}, which needs only the production run's own timing.

\subsection{Baselines and instruments}\label{sec:experiments-baselines}
Three comparators appear, each for a different question. The \emph{matched synchronous baseline} is pyABC \cite{klinger2018pyabc,schalte2022pyabc} with its acceptor replaced by a probabilistic-rejection acceptor using the same smooth kernel and the same bandwidth schedule as the asynchronous side (\S\ref{sec:implementation}); it is the method a practitioner would run, and the comparator for C2 and C3. The \emph{barrierized twin} is our own propagator with a collective barrier inserted before each proposal and nothing else changed (\S\ref{sec:implementation}); it isolates synchronization from every other difference and is the comparator for C1. A collective barrier needs identical call counts on every rank, so the twin runs to a fixed simulation count and the asynchronous arm to a fixed wall clock, and the two are compared as rates; for the posterior comparison the asynchronous arm is additionally re-run at exactly the twin's simulation count and seeds. \emph{Rejection ABC} spends the same simulation budget and keeps the $k$ best draws --- not a fixed threshold, which on an expensive simulator admits every draw --- and is given the whole machine (all $48$ ranks) rather than one rank per replicate. It is the reference for what an adaptive proposal buys per simulation.

\subsection{Metrics}\label{sec:experiments-metrics}
\paragraph{Tolerance at a matched budget.} The quantity that compares samplers on one footing is the $k$-th order statistic of a method's own discrepancies after $n$ simulations, $\epsilon_{(k)}(n)$: the tolerance at which exactly $k$ of its draws would be accepted. It needs no reference posterior, it does not depend on the bandwidth a schedule happened to report at, and it is what rejection ABC reports at by construction. Read at equal $n$ it measures per-simulation efficiency; read at the end of equal wall-clock budgets it measures the net effect; its slope in $\log n$ says whether more simulations keep converting into a tighter tolerance. We use $k=100$ throughout, count only records of completed simulations (the synchronous baseline also writes one record per accepted particle, and mixing the two flatters it), and order records by completion time.

\paragraph{Throughput, utilisation, and the predicted barrier cost.} Throughput is simulations completed per second of wall clock; utilisation is the fraction of worker time spent inside the simulator. The prediction that C1 tests is built from the asynchronous run only: with $D_i$ the per-evaluation durations on that run --- recorded simulation time plus the measured per-evaluation coordination overhead --- a barrierized generation of $W$ evaluations takes $\mathbb{E}[\max_{i\le W} D_i]$, so the synchronous throughput is predicted as $T_{\mathrm{sync}}=W/\mathbb{E}[\max_{i\le W}D_i]$, the expectation taken by resampling $W$ durations from the asynchronous record (or from the injected law, where the heterogeneity is injected; a persistently slow worker contributes its own mean). The ratio of the measured asynchronous throughput to that prediction is then compared with the ratio measured against the twin.

\paragraph{Posterior quality.} Calibration is checked by simulation-based calibration (SBC): empirical coverage of the reported posterior's central intervals over $1000$ trials at the $0.50/0.80/0.90/0.95$ levels. Where a reference posterior exists we report the Wasserstein distance $W_1$ between each method's \emph{own reported} posterior and that reference --- the analytic posterior for the Gaussian mean, and for g-and-k the posterior given the same octile summaries the discrepancy is built from, obtained from their asymptotic law (Appendix~\ref{app:protocol}); the asynchronous estimator is replayed over the prefix of the history completed by each checkpoint, the synchronous baseline is scored on its last completed generation with pyABC's population weights where the history was kept and uniformly otherwise, and rejection ABC on its $k$ best draws. On Cellular Potts, whose truth is known but whose posterior is not, we report each parameter's \emph{contraction} $1-\mathrm{sd}/\mathrm{sd}_{\mathrm{prior}}$ in prior-normalised units, whether the central $90\%$ interval covers the truth, and the effective sample size of the reported weights. We no longer report the distance from an unweighted archive to a point mass at the truth: that statistic is floored at the posterior's own spread and rewards a collapsed posterior over a correct one (Appendix~\ref{app:moved}).

\subsection{Studies}\label{sec:experiments-hpc}
C1 is tested on the straggler study (one of $16$ workers given a permanent post-evaluation delay of $0.1$\,s scaled by $0,1,5,10,20$), the heterogeneity study (every evaluation on $48$ workers multiplied by a lognormal factor of scale $\sigma\in\{0,0.5,1,1.5,2\}$), and the two Cellular Potts sizes, at $48$ to $384$ workers. C2 and C3 use the production runs of all four benchmarks at $48$ workers and fixed wall clock. C4 uses SBC on the Gaussian mean, a bimodal variant of it, and g-and-k, the reference-posterior recovery on the two benchmarks that admit one, and the Cellular Potts $50^3$ production runs, with and without the bandwidth defect described in \S\ref{sec:results-posterior}. Strong scaling to six and eight nodes, the hyperparameter sensitivity grid, the ablation of the kernel and of the AMIS reweighting, and the parameter-coupled-runtime study are reported in the supplement. All experiments ran on JUWELS (Appendix~\ref{app:protocol}); unless noted, results are medians over five replicates with the replicate range shown.

\section{Results}\label{sec:results}
Unless noted, results are five-replicate medians on JUWELS; figures compare the matched-kernel synchronous baseline (labelled \emph{Synchronous}) against the proposed asynchronous method (\emph{Asynchronous}, ours). We lead with the computational evidence (RQ2), the paper's central claim, then verify that inference quality is not sacrificed (RQ1), and close with the realistic workload (RQ3).

\subsection{Computational advantage (RQ2)}\label{sec:results-hpc}
\paragraph{Straggler robustness.} This is the clearest illustration of the barrier's cost (Fig.~\ref{fig:straggler}). One worker is given a permanent post-evaluation delay of $0.1$\,s scaled by $0\times$ (control)$,1\times,5\times,10\times,20\times$. As the injected delay grows from the control to $20\times$, the synchronous baseline's throughput collapses by more than two orders of magnitude (median $\approx 8800 \to 60$ simulations/s) because each generation blocks on the straggler, whereas the asynchronous method routes work to available workers and holds essentially flat ($\approx 3900 \to 3800$ simulations/s); only at the $0\times$ control is the baseline faster, where a generation barrier is cheap for this near-instant simulator. Posterior quality is not paid for this throughput: measured against the analytic posterior, the asynchronous method's reported estimator sits at $0.008$--$0.011$ at every slowdown and the baseline's final-population estimator at $0.017$--$0.027$ (\S\ref{sec:results-validity}). We read that gap as a difference in the estimators' support --- a weighted full history against a $100$-particle final generation --- rather than as a difference between the samplers.

\paragraph{Isolating synchronization: a barrierized twin.} The comparison above is against a different algorithm, so it cannot by itself attribute the gain to the barrier (\S\ref{sec:limitations}, item vii). We therefore build a \emph{barrierized twin} of our own method: the identical propagator --- same proposals, same kernel weights, same archive rule, same reported estimator --- with a collective barrier inserted immediately before each breed, so that the asynchronous arm and the twin differ in \emph{nothing but} when a worker is permitted to proceed. Table~\ref{tab:twin} reports both on the straggler benchmark over five replicates. Only the twin needs a simulation-count budget: a collective barrier requires identical per-rank call counts, so a wall-clock stop --- which halts ranks independently, as the first one to reach the deadline --- deadlocks it. The asynchronous arm therefore runs wall-limited ($300$\,s of active wall-clock) and the twin runs to a fixed simulation count, and we compare the two as \emph{rates}: simulations per second of active wall-clock on both arms. The asynchronous arm holds $\approx3100$--$3900$ simulations/s at every slowdown, reproducing the throughput of the main experiment above; the twin runs at $48.7$ simulations/s already at the $0\times$ control --- the cost of synchronizing a $\approx4$\,ms simulator, where barrier latency alone dominates --- and degrades to $8.0$ as the straggler worsens, a factor of $402$ below the asynchronous arm at $20\times$. Barrier removal is therefore worth between one and two-and-a-half orders of magnitude of throughput on this workload, measured against our own algorithm rather than inferred from a cross-algorithm comparison.

\emph{And posterior quality?} It needs more care than the throughput does, because the statistic this comparison first used has no resolution on this benchmark. $W_1$ between the unweighted top-$k$ archive and a point mass at the true parameter is indeed $0.069$--$0.085$ on all three arms at every slowdown. But the analytic posterior here has standard deviation $\sigma_{\mathrm{obs}}/\sqrt{n_{\mathrm{obs}}} = 0.1$, so an \emph{exactly correct} posterior scores $0.090$ on that statistic: every arm is at or just below the score of a perfect answer, and the ones below it are there because a point-mass target rewards an over-concentrated archive. Agreement on that number is not evidence of anything, and we no longer report it. Evaluated instead on the estimator this paper actually reports --- the full-history AMIS posterior, against the analytic posterior --- the arms separate sharply (Table~\ref{tab:twin}). Through $5\times$ all three agree at $0.008$--$0.015$. At $10\times$ the fine twin's reported posterior moves to $0.74$ and at $20\times$ to $1.73$, with the coarse twin following one step behind at $1.07$; the asynchronous arm stays at $0.009$--$0.013$ throughout. The twins' \emph{archives} stay on target in every one of these runs --- it is the weights that degenerate, not the particles.

We do not read this as the barrier corrupting the estimator, because the two arms are not at a matched budget: at $20\times$ the twin completes $3{,}200$ evaluations against the asynchronous arm's $959{,}000$ in the same active wall-clock --- ten generations against roughly nine hundred --- and importance weights formed over ten generations are expected to be poor whatever produced them. What the data support is the weaker conjunction: \emph{at a fixed wall-clock budget} the barrier costs one to two-and-a-half orders of magnitude of throughput, and on this workload that deficit is large enough to cost the reported posterior as well. Separating the barrier's own effect on the weights from the effect of the evaluations it forgoes needs a matched-evaluation-count twin, which we have not run (\S\ref{sec:limitations}).

\emph{Is the twin's fine generation doing the work?} The twin above synchronizes every $W{=}16$ evaluations, one per worker --- a finer generation than the synchronous baseline's population of $100$ --- so one could suspect the ratio is an artifact of barrier \emph{frequency} rather than of synchronization as such. We tested this directly by re-running the twin with the barrier coarsened to one per $112$ evaluations ($7W$, matching the baseline's population), five replicates at every slowdown (Table~\ref{tab:twin}, third row). Granularity turns out to matter only where the straggler does not: at the $0\times$ and $1\times$ controls the coarse twin is $3.3\times$ and $2.3\times$ faster ($48.7\to162.8$ and $65.3\to148.4$ simulations/s), cutting the asynchronous lead from $80\times$ to $24\times$ and from $48\times$ to $21\times$; but at $5\times$, $10\times$ and $20\times$ the two granularities agree to three significant figures ($31.6$ vs $31.8$, $15.91$ vs $15.94$, $7.98$ vs $7.98$), leaving the ratios at $102$--$402\times$. The mechanism is straightforward: a barrier costs either its own latency or the wait for the slowest member of the generation, whichever is larger. Coarsening amortizes the latency over more evaluations, which helps when latency dominates, but every generation still has to wait for the straggler no matter how long the generation is --- so in the straggler regime the cost is granularity-independent. The advantage we report under a straggler is thus not an artifact of the twin's schedule, and the remaining caveat is one of workload rather than granularity: this is a deliberately adversarial near-instant simulator, and where per-evaluation cost amortizes the coordination (\S\ref{sec:results-cpm}) the barrier's relative cost is correspondingly smaller.

\paragraph{The same test under runtime heterogeneity.} A persistent straggler is the sharpest case but not the typical one, so we repeat the twin comparison on the heterogeneity benchmark, where every worker draws a fresh lognormal runtime multiplier at every evaluation rather than one rank being permanently slow. Both barrier granularities again, five replicates at each of the five heterogeneity levels, simulation-limited with the budget per level sized from measured rates (Table~\ref{tab:twin-hetero}). The picture differs from the straggler case in two instructive ways. First, the $\sigma{=}0$ control now costs essentially \emph{nothing}: the twin runs at $44.8$ simulations/s against the asynchronous arm's $47.6$, a factor of $1.1$, where on the straggler benchmark the same control already cost $24$--$80\times$. The difference is the simulator, not the barrier --- these evaluations take ${\approx}1$\,s rather than ${\approx}4$\,ms, so barrier latency is negligible against the work it separates. The barrier's cost on a cost-bearing simulator is therefore a pure \emph{variance} cost, and it appears only once there is variance to pay for: it grows monotonically to $10.1\times$ at $\sigma{=}1.5$ and $25.3\times$ at $\sigma{=}2$ at the baseline-matched granularity ($30.9\times$ at the finer one). Second, coarsening the barrier now \emph{does} help --- $1.2$ to $1.7\times$ across $\sigma\ge0.5$, against the $1.00\times$ it bought under the persistent straggler. That contrast is the mechanism made visible: coarsening trades a heavier per-barrier maximum against half as many barriers, which is a favourable trade when the slow draw is transient and a different worker each time, and no trade at all when the same rank is slow in every generation. Posterior quality on these runs needs the same care as on the straggler benchmark, and for a different reason. The archives agree: the twin's top-$k$ archive recovers the analytic posterior mean to within $0.003$--$0.034$ absolute error against the asynchronous arm's $0.007$--$0.011$ (the synchronous baseline's final population, by contrast, sits at $0.012$--$0.139$), and in $W_1$ to the analytic posterior the archives are at $0.014$--$0.019$ and $0.015$--$0.106$ respectively. But this study is simulation-limited at $2112$ evaluations, and at that budget the \emph{reported} full-history estimator has not converged on \emph{any} arm: its $W_1$ to the analytic posterior is $0.19$--$2.34$ across the four arms, against $2.42$ for the prior itself. We therefore read these runs as what they were built to be --- a throughput comparison --- and take no posterior-quality claim from them beyond the archives agreeing. The two vendored columns are named accordingly.

There is one further reading worth stating, because it runs opposite to the concern the twin was built to address. The worry about a cross-algorithm comparison is that it might \emph{overstate} the barrier's role. Here it understates it: the synchronous pyABC baseline's penalty grows only from $2.1\times$ to $4.8\times$ over the same heterogeneity range, so at $\sigma{=}2$ our own barrierized twin is $5.3\times$ slower than the external baseline at matched barrier granularity. The design difference that accounts for it is the one noted in \S\ref{sec:limitations}, item iv: pyABC closes a generation on the first $100$ evaluations to finish and discards the latecomers, so it never waits for the slowest draw, whereas the twin --- like our own propagator, minus asynchrony --- keeps every evaluation and therefore pays the full maximum. The barrier's unmitigated cost is thus larger than the measured gap over pyABC, and the headline comparison is conservative rather than flattering.

\paragraph{Runtime heterogeneity.} Under stochastic log-normal runtime noise the asynchronous method sustains higher utilization (lower idle fraction) than the synchronous baseline, which idles at generation barriers (Fig.~\ref{fig:hetero}). Removing the barrier therefore preserves not just the simulation rate but the \emph{inference} delivered per unit wall-clock.

\paragraph{Parameter-coupled runtime (every-particle bias).} The study above slows simulations independently of their parameters; a sharper test couples runtime \emph{to the inferred parameter}, directly probing the every-particle bias of Limitation~(iv). On the Gaussian-mean benchmark ($48$ workers, $60$\,s budget, five replicates) we set the post-evaluation delay to $d(\mu)=\min\!\big(d_0\,e^{\,c(\mu-\mu_c)/\ell},\,d_{\max}\big)$ with base $d_0=0.02$\,s, centre $\mu_c=0$, length scale $\ell=0.15$, and cap $d_{\max}=0.5$\,s, and sweep the coupling strength $c\in\{0,0.5,1,2\}$ ($c=0$ recovers uniform runtime). Larger $c$ makes one region of parameter space evaluate far faster than its mirror image, so the asynchronous ``keep every accepted particle'' rule over-represents the fast region in the raw archive. The coupling has the intended effect: aggregate throughput falls as it steepens, by ${\approx}43\%$ for the asynchronous method from its peak (${\approx}1880/2010 \to 1150$ sims/s; it is flat between $c=0$ and $c=0.5$ and falls monotonically thereafter) and monotonically and more steeply for the barrier-bound baseline (${\approx}840 \to 250$; Fig.~\ref{fig:param-bias}a), confirming that the fast region dominates the raw sample counts. Yet the asynchronous posterior-mean error to the analytic Gaussian posterior does not increase as the coupling steepens; it stays ${\approx}0.01$ and comparable to the discard-latecomers synchronous baseline (Fig.~\ref{fig:param-bias}b). At the coupling levels tested the over-representation is measurable in the raw sampling rate but does not measurably distort the reported (unweighted top-$k$) posterior mean. A targeted ablation that removes the AMIS proposal adaptation ($S{=}0$) and re-runs the same coupling sweep leaves this robustness essentially unchanged --- the asynchronous posterior-mean error stays ${\approx}0.005$--$0.013$ with or without AMIS and shows no trend in the coupling --- which indicates that the raw archive is itself robust at these levels rather than that AMIS actively corrects the over-representation. Two finer tests that Limitation~(iv) motivates sharpen this rather than leave it open. First, we score the AMIS-\emph{reweighted} posterior --- the estimator the guarantees of \S\ref{sec:theory} concern, resampling the archive by the retroactive weight \eqref{eq:posterior-estimator} --- instead of the unweighted archive, and compare it against the no-AMIS variant ($S{=}0$) across the coupling sweep and a steeper regime (base delay $0.5$\,s, cap $8$\,s). The reweighted posterior recovers the analytic mean as accurately as the unweighted archive (absolute error ${\le}0.025$, no trend in the coupling) in both regimes and with or without AMIS; the reweighting's effect surfaces in the posterior \emph{spread}, not the mean --- it lowers the effective sample size (ESS fraction ${\approx}0.88$, vs ${\approx}0.99$ without AMIS), roughly triples the maximum normalised weight (${\approx}0.03$ vs ${\approx}0.01$), and widens the central $90\%$ interval by ${\approx}15\%$ --- so AMIS reshapes the tails but neither creates nor removes a mean bias at these coupling levels. Second, a drain-after-deadline variant lets every in-flight simulation finish past the wall-clock deadline and recomputes the posterior; comparing it against the censored run isolates the completion-time censoring that dividing by the proposal density does not correct. Draining moves the weighted posterior mean by at most $0.013$ --- comparable to the replicate-to-replicate spread (${\approx}0.01$) --- with no systematic direction, in both the mild and the steep regime. At these coupling levels the deadline censoring is therefore present in principle but negligible in effect, consistent with the sampler concentrating in the fast region near the posterior where few simulations are in flight at the deadline. We accordingly report the raw archive as robust here rather than claiming AMIS corrects the over-representation, and treat a formal censoring correction as future work (Limitation~(iv)).

\paragraph{Strong scaling.} Lotka--Volterra is deliberately an \emph{adversarial} simulator for a scaling study: each evaluation takes only $\approx 2$\,ms, so the simulator occupies only a small fraction of each worker's wall-clock and there is essentially no per-evaluation compute to distribute. A dedicated instrumented run measures this productive fraction directly (Fig.~\ref{fig:lv-timing}): at the $48$-worker throughput peak a worker spends only ${\approx}25\%$ of its time inside the simulator, leaving ${\approx}75\%$ for coordination and idle waiting, and that productive share falls to ${\approx}8\%$ and then ${\approx}2\%$ as the job spreads from one node to three to six. The mechanism is the method's single shared population: every completed evaluation is broadcast to all workers so that each can propose from the current archive, an all-to-all exchange whose per-arrival cost grows with the worker count. On a near-instantaneous simulator this exchange saturates and then actively slows the productive ranks. Aggregate throughput therefore rises almost linearly while the job fits on one node ($149\to369\to1983\to6240$ sims/s at $1\to4\to16\to48$ workers) and then \emph{declines} across fully packed multi-node jobs (worker counts that are exact multiples of $48$): $6240\to4195\to2615\to2088\to1729$ sims/s at $48\to144\to192\to240\to288$ workers, i.e.\ $1\to3\to4\to5\to6$ nodes, because adding ranks beyond one node lengthens the all-to-all exchange faster than it adds useful work. This is a property of coordinating a near-instantaneous simulator across a single shared population, not of asynchrony as such (precisely the regime in which asynchrony has least to offer), and it lifts once the simulator carries real per-evaluation cost that amortizes the coordination (the Cellular Potts result of \S\ref{sec:results-cpm}; Fig.~\ref{fig:scaling}, right). To keep the comparison fair at high core counts we give the synchronous ABC-SMC baseline a \emph{population size equal to the worker count} (a smaller population cannot occupy every core); it then uses all allocated cores and scales steadily, from ${\approx}440$ sims/s at $16$ workers to ${\approx}6000$ at $288$ (Table~\ref{tab:scaling}). On this free simulator the asynchronous method leads while the job stays within a few nodes (${\approx}4.5\times$ at $16$ workers, ${\approx}2\times$ at one full node), but its lead \emph{erodes} with scale and the fair synchronous baseline overtakes it beyond three nodes, where the growing all-to-all coordination cost dominates and the asynchronous method leaves most of the allocation idle. This is the honest boundary of the asynchronous advantage on a cheap, homogeneous simulator; it is largest exactly in the heterogeneous and straggler-prone regimes where barriers otherwise waste compute. The archive size sets where that boundary falls, because the per-arrival proposal reconstruction is $\mathcal{O}(k)$: enlarging the archive tenfold to $k=1000$ costs throughput at every worker count --- $39\%$ at the single-node peak ($6240\to3804$ sims/s) --- and brings the crossover with the fair baseline in from beyond three nodes to a single node (Table~\ref{tab:scaling}). Sweeping $k$ across $\{50,100,200,400,800,1000\}$ at every worker count resolves that cost into a smooth monotone decline in throughput and a single step in the crossover, which moves in from beyond three nodes to three nodes at $k\ge400$ (Table~\ref{tab:k-frontier}). The archive size is therefore a systems cost as well as a statistical knob --- but, put on the same axis as the calibration it buys, the two turn out \emph{not} to trade off over this range: the best-calibrated archive is also within $7\%$ of the fastest, and every archive larger than $k=100$ is both slower and worse calibrated (\S\ref{sec:results-validity}, Table~\ref{tab:k-frontier}).

\subsection{Posterior validity (RQ1)}\label{sec:results-validity}
Having established the systems advantage, we ask what it costs in inference quality. Across calibration and matched-budget posterior recovery the reported streaming posterior --- the full-history AMIS estimator \eqref{eq:posterior-estimator} --- is comparable to the matched synchronous baseline in low dimensions and under multimodality, and in four dimensions errs toward conservatism (it mildly over-covers) rather than over-confidence.

\paragraph{Calibration.} Table~\ref{tab:sbc} reports SBC empirical coverage on the Gaussian-mean model (1000 trials, matched simulation budget), for the reported full-history AMIS posterior. The asynchronous method tracks the nominal level closely ($0.51/0.80/0.90/0.94$ at the $0.50/0.80/0.90/0.95$ levels), within Monte Carlo error of nominal at every level; the synchronous baseline under-covers at every level ($0.35/0.63/0.75/0.83$), i.e.\ it is over-confident. At 1000 trials a coverage estimate carries a Monte Carlo standard error of only ${\approx}\,\pm0.007$--$0.016$, well below the ${\approx}0.12$--$0.17$ gap between the methods, so the ordering is resolvable rather than noise: on this benchmark the streaming estimator is \emph{better} calibrated than the matched synchronous baseline. Reporting the estimator over the full evaluated history rather than the top-$k$ archive alone matters even here: in a paired $500$-trial run that changes only the reported support, the truncated archive mildly under-covers at the top levels ($0.89/0.92$ at the $0.90/0.95$ levels), which the full-history report corrects to nominal --- a reporting-support effect that grows with dimension, as the multimodal and four-dimensional tests below make explicit. This test is on a one-dimensional model with an analytic posterior; the higher-dimensional and multimodal settings where the archive/proposal approximations are most likely to bite are probed separately below. Figure~\ref{fig:sbc} shows the corresponding coverage curve and rank histogram.

\paragraph{Calibration under multimodality and in higher dimensions.} To probe exactly those settings, we run SBC (1000 trials, matched simulation budget) on two harder targets, reporting the same full-history AMIS posterior. The first is a \emph{multimodal} model $y_i\sim\mathcal N(\theta^2,\sigma^2)$: the sign ambiguity gives a symmetric bimodal posterior at $\pm\sqrt{\bar y}$, the case a top-$k$ archive is most at risk of collapsing onto a single mode. It does not collapse --- the archive retains \emph{both} modes in $85.7\%$ of trials and the mode containing the true value in $86.7\%$ (the remainder are largely near-$\theta{=}0$ trials where the two modes merge), the importance weights stay near-uniform (median effective-sample-size fraction $0.99$, median maximum normalised weight $0.011$), and coverage tracks the nominal level ($0.50/0.82/0.91/0.96$), calibrated within Monte Carlo error. The second is the \emph{four-dimensional} g-and-k model, where the reporting-support choice bites. The reported full-history posterior is \emph{conservative}: it mildly over-covers at every level and parameter (e.g.\ empirical coverage $0.62/0.91/0.96/0.99$ at the $0.50/0.80/0.90/0.95$ levels for the location parameter $A$, and similarly for $B,g,k$), because the finite-$\epsilon$ smooth-ABC posterior it reports is over-dispersed at the loose bandwidth a four-dimensional budget reaches. The efficient top-$k$ archive used for online proposal sits at the opposite end: it is over-concentrated and would instead \emph{under}-cover ($0.85/0.89$ at the $0.90/0.95$ levels for $A$), with healthy weights throughout (median effective-sample-size fraction $0.81$), so the miscalibration is a reporting-support effect, not weight degeneracy. The calibrated posterior is therefore \emph{bracketed} between the over-concentrated archive core and the over-dispersed full history, and the amount of history reported is the dispersion knob: widening the reported support from the archive toward the full history raises coverage monotonically and continuously through nominal --- for $A$, $0.85/0.89 \to 0.93/0.96 \to 0.95/0.98 \to 0.97/0.99$ at the $0.90/0.95$ levels for reported sizes $M=200/500/1500/\text{full}$, crossing nominal near $M\approx400$--$800$ (the shape parameters $B$ and $k$ are already near-nominal at $M{=}500$, e.g.\ $0.89/0.94$ for $B$). This is a property of \emph{how much of the history is reported}, not of the AMIS weighting (whose importance weights stay well-conditioned) or of the budget: doubling the simulation budget to $80{,}000$ per trial leaves the archive coverage essentially unchanged ($0.85/0.89$ at the $0.90/0.95$ levels for $A$, within Monte Carlo error for $B,g,k$). We accordingly report the streaming estimator, evaluated over the full evaluated history, as well calibrated in one dimension and under multimodality, and conservative --- over-covering rather than over-confident --- in four dimensions; an intermediate reported support ($M\approx400$--$800$) or a larger archive tighten the four-dimensional report toward exactly nominal.

\paragraph{How calibration depends on the archive size and the snapshot buffer.} The tests above fix $k$ and $S$ per benchmark, which leaves open whether the two efficiency knobs must be retuned as the dimension grows. To settle this we sweep them directly on a $d$-dimensional Gaussian-mean target, $y_i\sim\mathcal N(\theta,\sigma^2 I_d)$ with $\theta\in\mathbb R^d$ under a flat box prior, whose posterior is known exactly at every $d$ (SBC against that exact posterior returns $0.496/0.794/0.905/0.952$, confirming the target itself is calibrated, so any departure below is a property of the method). The grid is $d\in\{1,2,4,8,16\}\times k\in\{50,100,200,400,800\}\times S\in\{0,5,20,50\}$, $1000$ trials per cell, reporting the same full-history estimator (Table~\ref{tab:ks-sweep}).

The two knobs behave quite differently. The snapshot buffer $S$ has a threshold and then saturates: at $S=0$ the estimator is over-confident in low dimensions (mean deviation $-0.088$ at $d=1,2$), $S=5$ removes essentially all of it ($-0.001$), and $S=20$ and $S=50$ add nothing further --- at any dimension. The value of a richer balance-heuristic denominator therefore does \emph{not} grow with $d$ over this range, and the fixed $S=20$ we use throughout sits comfortably above the knee. The archive size $k$ is the opposite: its effect is large, strongly dimension-dependent, and \emph{not} monotone. Small archives can be severely over-confident in intermediate dimensions --- at $d=8,k=50$ empirical coverage is $0.29/0.52/0.63/0.71$ against the nominal $0.50/0.80/0.90/0.95$, a failure that persists at every $S$ --- while intermediate archives over-cover in the same regime ($+0.18$ to $+0.20$ at $k=200$--$400$). We note that this does not follow the floor a naive count of covariance parameters would predict: the perturbation covariance is a full $d\times d$ matrix estimated from the archive, suggesting $k\gtrsim d(d{+}1)/2$, yet $d=8$ fails at $k=50>36$ while $d=16$ remains within $0.06$ of nominal at $k=50<136$. The reading this supports is that $d=16$ is protected by its own difficulty: with $20{,}000$ simulations over a $[-5,5]^{16}$ prior the sampler barely concentrates, so the reported posterior stays broad and covers almost by default, whereas $d=8$ has enough signal to concentrate but not enough to concentrate correctly. Two further runs test that reading rather than leaving it as narrative. First, it predicts that a \emph{higher} dimension should look benign again: at $d=32$ the coverage is $0.55$--$0.57$, $0.83$--$0.84$, $0.92$, $0.96$ at the four levels for every $k\in\{50,100,800\}$ --- mildly conservative and nowhere near the $d=8$ failure, even at $k=50$. Second, the failure is not a budget shortfall: doubling the simulation budget at $d=8,k=50$ makes coverage slightly \emph{worse} ($0.26/0.47/0.58/0.67$ against $0.29/0.52/0.63/0.71$ at the original budget), exactly as expected if the extra simulations sharpen an already mislocated posterior rather than repairing it. We therefore do not offer a closed-form rule for $k$; the practical guidance is that the archive size is the dispersion knob (\S\ref{sec:results-validity}), that its safe range is problem- and dimension-specific, and that it should be chosen by checking calibration directly rather than by a formula.

\paragraph{The archive size does not, in this range, trade calibration against throughput.} Because $k$ has both a statistical effect (above) and a systems cost --- per-arrival proposal reconstruction is $\mathcal{O}(k)$, so a larger archive is slower at every worker count (\S\ref{sec:results-hpc}) --- it is natural to expect the two to pull against each other, and to have to buy calibration with throughput. Measuring both on one axis shows that expectation is wrong over the range we can test. Table~\ref{tab:k-frontier} puts the throughput cost of $k$ next to its worst-case miscalibration over the dimensions swept, using a throughput grid matched on everything but $k$ (Lotka--Volterra, $180$\,s wall cap, five replicates, worker counts $1$--$288$: $k\in\{100,1000\}$ from the main scaling run and $k\in\{50,200,400,800\}$ from a dedicated $k$-frontier run sharing that configuration). Of the six archive sizes, only two are Pareto-optimal, and they are the two smallest: $k=100$ is simultaneously the best-calibrated setting tested (worst deviation $0.064$, at $d=4$) and within $7\%$ of the fastest ($6240$ vs $6684$ simulations/s at the single-node peak). Every larger archive is dominated by it on \emph{both} axes at once --- $k=200$ is $5\%$ slower and nearly three times worse calibrated ($0.180$), $k=400$ is $14\%$ slower and $0.195$, and $k=800$ costs $31\%$ of throughput to end up still slightly worse calibrated than $k=100$ ($0.074$). The only genuinely faster archive, $k=50$, is the catastrophic one ($0.250$ at $d=8$). So the practical shape of the $k$ decision is not a frontier to negotiate but a floor to clear: go far enough above the small-archive failure region, and then stop, because further enlargement buys no calibration and costs throughput monotonically.

Two qualifications keep this from being read too broadly. The join is on $k$ alone and the two axes come from different benchmarks by design --- calibration needs a target whose posterior is known at every $d$, throughput needs the regime where an $\mathcal{O}(k)$ per-arrival cost is most exposed. That second choice makes the throughput column an \emph{upper} bound on the relative cost of $k$: Lotka--Volterra evaluations take ${\approx}2$\,ms, so coordination dominates there, and on a cost-bearing simulator the per-evaluation work amortizes the archive operation (\S\ref{sec:results-cpm}). The conclusion is therefore conservative in the right direction --- even where $k$ is most expensive, the best-calibrated archive is nearly free. What $k$ \emph{does} change at scale is where the fair synchronous baseline overtakes the asynchronous method, and there it acts as a step rather than a smooth trade: the crossover sits beyond three fully packed nodes for $k\le200$ and moves in to three nodes for $k\ge400$ (last column).

\paragraph{Archive concentration.} We first report the top-$k$ concentration diagnostic, on all three benchmarks and for completeness with the earlier literature, and then turn to the estimator the paper reports. Read as what it is --- distance from the archive to a point mass, which rewards concentration whether or not the posterior is right (\S\ref{sec:experiments-suite}) --- it says the following. On g-and-k the asynchronous method concentrates slightly \emph{tighter} than the synchronous baseline at matched wall-clock budget ($0.078$ vs.\ $0.090$, inter-quartile ranges $[0.077,0.083]$ and $[0.082,0.093]$), and on Lotka--Volterra the two are comparable with the asynchronous method marginally tighter ($0.316$ vs.\ $0.332$, overlapping inter-quartile ranges); on Lotka--Volterra both numbers rest on the ${\approx}2\%$ of simulations that do not go extinct (\S\ref{sec:experiments-suite}). On the expensive Cellular Potts simulator the ordering reverses and the asynchronous method is \emph{modestly but consistently behind}: the async$-$sync difference is positive across $87.5\%$ of the budget, ending at ${+}0.043$ with an inter-quartile envelope of $[0.019,0.052]$ that excludes zero, and it never exceeds ${\approx}0.06$ against a Wasserstein scale of ${\approx}0.4$ (Figs.~\ref{fig:posterior-recovery}c and~\ref{fig:cpm-diff}). One property of this benchmark bounds how that gap should be read: the Wasserstein-to-reference \emph{rises} with compute for both methods (asynchronous $0.37\to0.48$), because the Cellular Potts parameters are only weakly identified (\S\ref{sec:results-cpm}) and the comparison is against a reference point rather than a known posterior, so a diffuse cloud scores better than a posterior that has concentrated slightly off that reference. The metric therefore penalizes concentration on this target, and the asynchronous method --- which completes ${\approx}2\times$ the evaluations in the same budget --- concentrates fastest. Per-parameter posteriors and joint marginals are consistent with the known posteriors on the benchmarks that have one, and on Cellular Potts they are consistent for one parameter of two: the motility reference sits inside the central $90\%$ of both methods' marginals (at their $87$th and $91$st percentiles), whereas the division-rate reference falls below \emph{every} one of the $500$ reported draws for the asynchronous method and for the synchronous baseline alike (5th percentiles $0.19$ and $0.21$ against a reference of $0.05$). Both methods therefore exclude the division-rate reference outright rather than merely failing to resolve it, and rejection ABC places it only at its own $5$th percentile while remaining nearly uniform. This is the identifiability property of the benchmark asserting itself and it is common to all three methods (\S\ref{sec:results-cpm}); it is the reason no quality claim on Cellular Potts should be read as a bound on attainable accuracy. On the Gaussian-mean benchmark the asynchronous method recovers the posterior mean to within $\approx0.002$--$0.038$ absolute error, comparable to the synchronous baseline's $\approx0.006$--$0.021$ and far tighter than a rejection-ABC reference; the SBC calibration above is unaffected.

\paragraph{Scoring the reported estimator against a reference posterior.} The curves above measure the top-$k$ archive against a point mass at the true parameter, which is a concentration diagnostic and not posterior quality (\S\ref{sec:experiments-suite}). On the two benchmarks that admit a reference posterior we can do better and score the estimator this paper actually reports --- the retroactive AMIS posterior of \eqref{eq:posterior-estimator}, the object Theorem~\ref{thm:consistency} is about --- against that reference. We do it over time by replaying \texttt{extract\_posterior} on the prefix of the history that had completed by each checkpoint, which is what the run would have reported had it stopped there; the synchronous baseline is scored on its last completed generation and rejection ABC on its accepted set, so each method is judged on its own reported object (Fig.~\ref{fig:reported-recovery}). The reference is the analytic posterior for Gaussian-mean, and for g-and-k the posterior given the same seven octile summaries the discrepancy is built from --- \emph{not} the full-data posterior, which would measure the summaries' information loss rather than the sampler (Appendix~\ref{app:protocol}).

Two things come out of it, and the second was not what we went looking for.

\emph{First, the estimator recovers the posterior in one dimension and is beaten in four.} On Gaussian-mean the reported AMIS posterior sits at $W_1 = 0.008$--$0.016$ from the analytic posterior across the $300$\,s budget, against the synchronous baseline's $0.019$--$0.028$ and rejection ABC's $2.35$: the streaming estimator is about twice as accurate as the baseline's final population at every checkpoint. On g-and-k the ordering reverses and the margin is similar in size the other way. The baseline holds $0.021$--$0.043$ against the reference posterior over the $600$\,s budget and ends at $0.029$ (inter-quartile range $[0.021,0.029]$); the asynchronous method ranges over $0.042$--$0.296$ and ends at $0.069$ ($[0.037,0.073]$), with both far below rejection ABC's $1.01$. We report this plainly because the concentration diagnostic says the opposite on the same benchmark --- there the asynchronous archive is the tighter of the two ($0.078$ vs.\ $0.090$) --- and the disagreement is the point of measuring the reported estimator rather than the archive.

This is also a claim the point-mass metric was incapable of supporting in either direction: on the Gaussian configuration it floors at $0.090$, which is what an \emph{exactly correct} posterior scores, and every method sat at or below it (\S\ref{sec:results-hpc}).

\emph{Second, the effective sample size does not grow with the budget.} The reported posterior's ESS is $237$--$297$ particles at every checkpoint of the Gaussian run while the evaluated history grows from $1.0\times10^5$ to $1.3\times10^6$; the run's own stored weights give $261$--$303$ across the five replicates. Thirteen times the compute buys no additional effective particles, which is also why the accuracy curve above is flat rather than improving. The mechanism is not asynchrony --- it reproduces in a serial driver --- and it is not a defect of the replay. It is the interaction between where the estimator is reported and how the bandwidth is chosen: the posterior is reported at the tightest bandwidth the run reached, that bandwidth is set by ESS-retention bisection against the archive and therefore tracks the archive downwards, and so the set of particles carrying non-negligible kernel weight stays at a few multiples of $k$ while everything outside it is weighted to near zero. A controlled sweep makes the scaling explicit: over $k \in \{50,100,200\}$ and $n \in \{5\times10^3, 2\times10^4, 8\times10^4\}$, $\mathrm{ESS}/k$ stays within $1.9$--$3.6$ at all nine points while $\mathrm{ESS}/n$ falls like $1/n$. The full history enters the mixture denominator; it does not, in any effective sense, enter the numerator.

Dimension changes the character of this, and it is what explains the g-and-k result above. In one dimension the effective sample size is not just small but \emph{stable}: $237$--$297$ at every checkpoint of every replicate, and accuracy is correspondingly steady, with no relationship between the two (log--log correlation $0.04$). In four dimensions it is small \emph{and} erratic --- across the five g-and-k replicates it ranges from $7$ to $2987$ at the same points in wall-clock time, moving by two orders of magnitude between neighbouring checkpoints of a single run --- and accuracy follows it closely: the log--log correlation between effective sample size and $W_1$ to the reference posterior is $-0.80$. Every excursion above $W_1 = 0.3$ in that panel is a checkpoint where the effective sample had collapsed below $50$. So the higher-dimensional deficit is not a bias in the estimator but intermittent weight degeneracy in the cumulative-mixture denominator, and it is the same effect the calibration study meets as an under-covering top-$k$ report and an over-covering full-history one (\S\ref{sec:results-validity}), seen here as variance rather than as coverage.

The condition for the $\mathrm{ESS} \approx 3k$ ceiling is that the bandwidth actually tightens, which is to say that the target is identified --- and the Cellular Potts run is the instructive counter-case. There the reported posterior retains an ESS \emph{fraction} of $0.21$ over $33{,}748$ evaluations, i.e.\ ${\approx}7{,}200$ effective particles against the Gaussian run's ${\approx}280$, because the parameters confound (\S\ref{sec:results-cpm}), the bandwidth stalls, and almost every particle keeps weight. The two cases are the same mechanism read at its two ends, and neither is good news on its own: an identified target concentrates the effective support onto a few multiples of $k$, and a confounded one keeps a large effective sample of an uninformative posterior. This is the same object as the stabilization rate of \S\ref{sec:theory-r}, seen from the estimator's side rather than the bandwidth's: what governs it is the identifiability of the target, not the sampler.

We think this is the single most useful thing to know about the method, and it reframes three results we had reported separately. It is why reporting over the full history rather than the top-$k$ archive helps calibration but only up to a point, and why an intermediate reported support of $M\approx400$--$800$ was where coverage crossed nominal: that is $4$--$8k$, about where the effective support already sits. It is consistent with the measured failure of the CLT's rate condition for the reported configuration (\S\ref{sec:theory-r}): an asymptotic variance that shrinks at rate $n$ is not available from an effective sample that does not grow with $n$. And it says plainly where the method's remaining headroom is --- in $k$ and in the bandwidth rule, not in more workers. The throughput results of \S\ref{sec:results-hpc} stand on their own as systems results; what they buy in posterior accuracy, on a benchmark this well specified, is bounded by an effective support that more simulation alone does not enlarge.

\subsection{Realistic simulator: Cellular Potts (RQ3)}\label{sec:results-cpm}
The Cellular Potts benchmark runs end-to-end with the real \texttt{cellsInSilico} simulator, a realistic, expensive, heterogeneous-runtime workload and the regime the method targets.

\paragraph{Strong scaling.} Because each Cellular Potts evaluation carries real cost, the per-arrival coordination that capped Lotka--Volterra is amortized, and the asynchronous method scales almost linearly all the way to eight nodes (Fig.~\ref{fig:scaling}, right): median throughput rises from $0.2$ to $72.0$ simulations/s over $1$ to $384$ workers and, unlike the near-instantaneous Lotka--Volterra case, keeps scaling \emph{across} the single-node$\to$multi-node boundary, each node-doubling roughly doubling throughput ($9.3\to18.7\to37.8\to72.0$ sims/s at $48\to96\to192\to384$ workers), for $\approx 97\%$ parallel efficiency relative to the single-node rate. The synchronous baseline, given a population equal to the worker count so it too can occupy every core, scales only \emph{sub-linearly} over the same range ($3.8\to4.5\to7.8\to14.2$ sims/s at $48\to96\to192\to384$ workers, ${\approx}50\%$ efficiency), so at $384$ workers the asynchronous method sustains ${\approx}5\times$ its throughput, a gap that \emph{widens} with scale. This is the regime the method is built for: an expensive, heterogeneous simulator where the generation barrier, not per-arrival overhead, is the binding cost. Worker utilization makes the mechanism explicit (Fig.~\ref{fig:cpm-util}): the asynchronous method keeps workers ${\approx}98.9$--$99.8\%$ busy across all scales, whereas the synchronous baseline, even with every core filled within a generation, then waits for the slowest simulation at the barrier, so its utilization falls to ${\approx}24$--$29\%$ ($41\%\to24\%\to26\%\to29\%$ from $48$ to $384$ workers, i.e.\ ${\approx}70$--$76\%$ idle at every multi-node scale). This barrier idle, not per-arrival overhead, is what holds the synchronous throughput to sub-linear scaling while the asynchronous method tracks the ideal. (We report medians throughout.)

\paragraph{The barrierized twin at this scale.} The twin comparison of \S\ref{sec:results-hpc} isolates synchronization on the two synthetic benchmarks; here we run it on the real simulator, at the same four worker counts and the same $k{=}100$, simulation-limited at $40$ evaluations per worker (Table~\ref{tab:twin-cpm}). Two things come out of it, and the second is the more interesting.

First, the barrier alone costs a factor that is remarkably \emph{stable} across scale: $1.9\times$ at one, two and four nodes, rising to $2.8\times$ at eight. The external pyABC baseline's penalty over the same range grows much faster, from $2.4\times$ to $5.1\times$. The twin is therefore consistently the faster of the two synchronous arms, and the distance between them widens with scale --- which is the honest answer to the question the twin was built to ask, and it is not the flattering one. On this workload the barrier is a \emph{major but not exclusive} cause of the cross-algorithm gap: it accounts for roughly half to three-quarters of it in log terms, and the residual, which grows with worker count, has to come from pyABC's other differences (its probabilistic-rejection step, its classical population weighting, and its own coordination costs) rather than from synchronization. We therefore do \emph{not} claim the Cellular Potts gap is attributable to the barrier alone --- only that the barrier is the single largest identified component of it. This is also the opposite ordering to the straggler and heterogeneity benchmarks, where the twin ran far \emph{slower} than pyABC because pyABC's latecomer discarding rescues it from waiting on the slowest draw; with no injected pathology, that rule has little to exploit and our more efficient propagator keeps the twin ahead.

Second --- and this is the more useful finding --- the barrier does not merely reduce throughput, it makes throughput \emph{unpredictable}. The asynchronous arm's five replicates agree to within $1.3\%$ at every scale (coefficient of variation $0.6$--$1.3\%$), while the twin's spread across the \emph{same} seeds is $25$--$54\%$: at $48$ workers its replicates run at $1.7$, $2.1$, $4.9$, $5.9$ and $6.0$ simulations/s, a factor of $3.5$ between fastest and slowest. The mechanism is the one the utilization measurement already shows: a generation ends only when its slowest evaluation does, so the whole job inherits the cost of the most expensive parameter draw in each generation, and on this benchmark simulation cost varies strongly with the parameters. Asynchrony removes that coupling --- a slow draw delays only its own worker --- which is why the asynchronous arm's throughput is reproducible to within a percent while its barrierized twin, running identical proposals from identical seeds, varies by more than threefold. For a practitioner this is arguably the more consequential of the two effects: a median slowdown can be budgeted for, a factor-of-three variance in time-to-result cannot.

One note on the twin as an instrument. It intermittently deadlocks at $\ge192$ ranks --- we observed five hangs while collecting this table, one in the MPI teardown and the others mid-run --- so individual replicates had to be retried. Every combination did complete, and all twenty entries here are full runs; but the twin is a diagnostic built for this comparison rather than a robust implementation, and at these rank counts it needs supervision.

\paragraph{Posterior quality.} On the inference side the asynchronous method produces marginals of similar shape to the synchronous baseline (Fig.~\ref{fig:cpm-posterior}), though it trails modestly on distance-to-reference (\S\ref{sec:results-validity}): the cell-motility parameter concentrates near its reference value, while the division rate is not merely unresolved but mislocated --- every method, the baseline and rejection ABC included, places its mass away from the reference (\S\ref{sec:results-validity}). That is a property of the configuration rather than an incidental finding: this benchmark was built to supply a realistic heterogeneous-runtime workload for the scaling and throughput measurements, and its summaries, discrepancy metric and parameter ranges were never selected for identifiability, with the two parameters confounding. Quality comparisons on it should be read as evidence that no method is advantaged by the setup, not as a measurement of what any of them can achieve. This weak identification is a structural property of the inference problem, not of asynchrony: at this scale the division-rate and motility parameters are near-degenerate (both chiefly modulate the cell count that the summaries resolve), so neither method separates them and both improve only modestly on rejection ABC. The matched comparison therefore isolates the systems advantage as largely \emph{orthogonal} to identifiability: both methods are limited chiefly by the problem. On the summary metric the asynchronous method is modestly behind over the full budget (${+}0.043$ in Wasserstein-to-reference against a scale of ${\approx}0.4$; \S\ref{sec:results-validity}), so we report its throughput advantage as bought at a small quality cost on this benchmark rather than at none --- with the caveat that the metric rewards dispersion on a weakly identified target, and that the asynchronous method, concentrating fastest, is the one it penalizes most. Establishing the same quality with a synchronous matched baseline at the still larger scales the method targets would itself be prohibitively wasteful, precisely the cost the barrier-free design removes.

\subsection{Ablation}\label{sec:results-ablation}
Reverting the smooth kernel to a hard indicator slightly raises the Wasserstein-to-truth relative to the full method (though within its confidence interval), while removing the AMIS cumulative-mixture reweighting is approximately neutral on this uniform-runtime analytic target (Fig.~\ref{fig:ablation}). On the Gaussian-mean benchmark the full method attains a final Wasserstein-to-truth of $0.073$ (CI $[0.067,0.079]$); the hard-indicator kernel raises it to $0.076$ (inside that interval), whereas dropping AMIS leaves it essentially unchanged ($0.072$; Fig.~\ref{fig:ablation}a), with the remaining hyperparameter variants scattered around the full-method reference and degrading only toward the largest perturbation scales ($0.084$--$0.087$). That AMIS is neutral here is consistent with the coupling study (\S\ref{sec:results-hpc}), where removing AMIS likewise left the posterior essentially unchanged: with no runtime--parameter coupling on this uniform-runtime target, there is no over-representation for the reweighting to reshape. The AMIS-isolation trajectory (Fig.~\ref{fig:ablation}b) accordingly shows the full method and the no-AMIS variant tracking each other throughout the converged regime (both $\approx 0.072$--$0.073$ after $40$\,s).

\subsection{Sensitivity}\label{sec:results-sensitivity}
Across the full hyperparameter grid \emph{on the Gaussian-mean benchmark} (the analytic-posterior sanity model) the asynchronous method's posterior quality is stable, with no region of catastrophic failure (Fig.~\ref{fig:sensitivity}). The Wasserstein distance to the true parameter stays within $\approx0.09$--$0.15$ across the grid (each cell averaged over five replicates and the two smooth kernels); the archive size $k$ matters only marginally \emph{for point quality at this dimension} (its effect on calibration in higher dimensions is a separate and much larger story, Table~\ref{tab:ks-sweep}), and quality degrades only mildly toward the largest perturbation scales and initial tolerances. The tolerance scheduler is inert under a smooth kernel --- the asynchronous selector chooses $\epsilon$ by ESS-retention bisection regardless of the scheduler type --- so we fixed it to the acceptance-rate rule rather than sweeping a variable that cannot change the smooth-kernel result. We therefore did not find delicate tuning necessary to reach the quality reported above \emph{on this benchmark}. We did not repeat the full grid on the costlier non-Gaussian benchmarks, so this robustness statement is scoped to the analytic-posterior model and to the qualitative trends observed there (scheduler-insensitive; mild, monotone sensitivity to perturbation scale and initial tolerance) rather than asserted as a general guarantee. Two further scope limits matter. This grid measures \emph{point quality} (Wasserstein to the truth) in \emph{one} dimension; it says nothing about calibration, and the archive size that looks inconsequential here is decisive for coverage in higher dimensions --- the dimension-resolved sweep of \S\ref{sec:results-validity} (Table~\ref{tab:ks-sweep}) finds a genuinely catastrophic region ($d=8$, $k=50$: coverage $0.29$ at the $0.50$ level) that a one-dimensional quality grid cannot see. ``No failure region'' should therefore be read as a statement about one-dimensional point quality only.

\section{Discussion}\label{sec:discussion}
The results support the three claims. Asynchronous execution helps most where it should: under a persistent straggler and under runtime heterogeneity, the generation barrier is the dominant cost and removing it preserves throughput that the synchronous baseline loses. For homogeneous, near-instantaneous simulators at large worker counts the asynchronous coordination overhead does not pay off: the multi-node Lotka--Volterra points, where the fair synchronous baseline overtakes the asynchronous method, make this honest boundary explicit. On the realistic Cellular Potts workload (the cost-bearing regime the design targets) the method scales almost linearly while the synchronous baseline, held at the generation barrier even when given a matching population, scales only sub-linearly --- at a small measured cost in posterior quality on that benchmark ($\S$\ref{sec:results-validity}), on a metric that penalizes the concentration the extra throughput buys.

\section{Limitations}\label{sec:limitations}
We are explicit about what \S\ref{sec:theory} does and does not give. (i) AMIS estimators are consistent but not finite-sample unbiased, since proposals depend on past particles; a local-decision rule in the spirit of \cite{paige2014cascade} that recovers finite-sample unbiasedness for ABC is future work. (ii) The bandwidth floor $\epsilon_\infty>0$ in Assumption~\ref{ass:stab} means the theorems characterize the smooth-ABC posterior at the limit bandwidth, not the exact posterior at $\epsilon=0$; and for the central limit theorem (though not for consistency) that limit must be \emph{deterministic}, since the centred integrand it is built on would otherwise vary with the run. Setting \texttt{min\_tol} bounds the limit below but only a schedule that reaches its floor makes it deterministic. The reported runs leave \texttt{min\_tol} unset and use the data-driven schedule, so neither holds, and the bandwidth a finite budget reaches is not an asymptotic limit at all. (iii) The theorems hold up to the limiting ratio $r$ of \eqref{eq:fidelity} between the density the sample was drawn from and the function the estimator divides by; $r\equiv1$ gives the smooth-ABC posterior exactly, and \S\ref{sec:theory-r} decomposes $r$ into four factors and \emph{measures two of them} --- the prior floor and the snapshot quadrature --- at $\widehat\zeta=0.022$ on the Cellular Potts production run, a $1.1\%$ shift in total variation against a large-$m$ reference. The other two, the truncation constant and the capped-rejection/redraw conditioning, are bounded rather than measured, and because the factors multiply that is a partial accounting rather than a bound on the whole. We do not prove $r\equiv1$. Under asynchronous execution one of the four factors additionally absorbs the difference between the emitted proposal density and the conditional law under a filtration the proofs can use (Assumption~\ref{ass:filtration}), which is a property of the schedule rather than of the estimator. The stabilization clause (Assumption~\ref{ass:stab}(ii)) is not merely unproved for the top-$k$ archive rule but measurably violated in the configuration we run: the proposal's distance to its limit decays far too slowly (\S\ref{sec:theory-r}), so the central limit theorem covers the damped- or frozen-adaptation variants rather than these experiments, while consistency, which needs no rate, is unaffected. (iv) Keeping every accepted particle (unlike pyABC's discard-latecomers rule) over-represents fast-simulator parameter regions in the raw archive. The parameter-coupled-runtime study (\S\ref{sec:results-hpc}, Fig.~\ref{fig:param-bias}) quantifies this: steepening the coupling cuts throughput as the fast region comes to dominate the raw counts, yet --- across the sweep, a steeper regime, and with or without AMIS --- neither the unweighted posterior mean nor the AMIS-reweighted posterior's mean drifts with the coupling (AMIS reshapes the tails, not the mean), and a drain-after-deadline test finds the completion-time censoring negligible in effect at these levels (numbers there). We therefore do not correct for it algorithmically and do not claim the AMIS reweighting cancels it: dividing by the proposal density corrects proposal adaptation, not selection by completion time, so a formal correction would need completion probabilities or an anytime-sampling argument, and a residual finite-sample effect at stronger couplings cannot be ruled out --- both left to future work. (v) The snapshot buffer is fixed at $S=20$; adaptive sizing is a natural extension. (vi) Simulation-based calibration of the reported posterior --- the full-history AMIS estimator \eqref{eq:posterior-estimator} --- shows it well calibrated in one dimension and robust to multimodality (retaining both modes of a bimodal target in ${\approx}86\%$ of trials), and conservative rather than over-confident on the four-dimensional g-and-k model, where it mildly \emph{over}-covers. This four-dimensional behaviour is a reporting-support effect, not a weighting or budget failure (\S\ref{sec:results-validity}): the efficient top-$k$ archive used for online proposal is over-concentrated and would under-cover, the full evaluated history is over-dispersed and over-covers, and coverage rises monotonically through nominal as the reported support grows from one to the other, so the calibrated posterior is bracketed between the two. We report the conservative full-history end by default; an intermediate reported support or a larger archive would tighten the four-dimensional report to nominal. The denominator's prior floor acts in the same direction for a different reason, and the two are worth not conflating: the floor suppresses the far tails of the reported posterior (\S\ref{sec:theory-r}(a)) and so tightens it, whereas widening the reported support adds tail mass. Both are small next to the effect measured here --- the floor and the snapshot quadrature together move the reported posterior by about one percent in total variation (\S\ref{sec:theory-r}). (vi-b) On an identified target the reported posterior's effective sample size is set by the archive size and not by the simulation budget: $\mathrm{ESS}/k \in [1.9, 3.6]$ across a sweep of $k$ and $n$, and $237$--$303$ effective particles out of $1.1$--$1.3\times10^6$ evaluated on the production Gaussian run (\S\ref{sec:results-validity}). The bound is on \emph{identified} targets specifically --- the confounded Cellular Potts target retains an ESS fraction of $0.21$, because its bandwidth never tightens enough to concentrate the weights --- so it binds exactly where the posterior is worth having. Barrier-free execution therefore delivers more \emph{evaluations} per second, which is what \S\ref{sec:results-hpc} measures, but converting those into posterior accuracy is limited by a reporting rule we did not design for that purpose. Raising $k$ raises the effective sample proportionally and is the obvious lever; a bandwidth rule that does not track the archive downwards, or reporting at a deliberately looser $\epsilon$ than the tightest reached, are the others. We have not swept any of them for this purpose, and until that is done the honest scope of the systems result is throughput rather than time-to-accuracy. (vii) The synchronous baseline is matched on the ABC kernel and bandwidth schedule, but it still differs from our method in more than the barrier: it adds a probabilistic-rejection step, its generation length depends on the resulting acceptance probability, and it retains classical population weighting rather than the streaming estimator. Matching the kernel removes the acceptance \emph{rule} as a confound, and the \emph{barrierized twin} of \S\ref{sec:results-hpc} (Table~\ref{tab:twin}) closes the remaining gap on the straggler benchmark: the same propagator with a collective barrier before each breed runs $21$--$402\times$ slower than the asynchronous arm, so on that workload the throughput gain is attributable to synchronization rather than merely dominated by it. The twin does \emph{not} isolate synchronization for the posterior comparison, only for the throughput one: a collective barrier needs identical per-rank call counts, so the twin cannot be stopped on wall-clock and the two arms end at budgets that differ by two orders of magnitude (\S\ref{sec:results-hpc}). A matched-evaluation-count twin, which would separate the barrier's effect on the importance weights from the effect of the evaluations it forgoes, is the obvious next control and we have not run it. The granularity objection --- that the twin synchronizes every $W$ evaluations, a finer generation than the baseline's population of $100$ --- is measured rather than assumed away: re-running the twin with the barrier coarsened to the baseline's population changes its throughput by less than $1\%$ at every slowdown from $5\times$ up, and matters ($2.3$--$3.3\times$) only at the controls where barrier latency and not straggler waiting is the bottleneck (Table~\ref{tab:twin}). The twin has since been repeated under runtime heterogeneity (Table~\ref{tab:twin-hetero}), where it costs $1.1\times$ at the $\sigma{=}0$ control and $25.3\times$ at $\sigma{=}2$ at the baseline-matched granularity, so the attribution now covers both synthetic HPC pathologies rather than only the sharpest one. That test also shows the cross-algorithm comparison to be conservative in the direction that matters: pyABC's own penalty grows only to $4.8\times$, because it discards latecomers instead of waiting for the slowest draw (item iv), so the barrier's unmitigated cost exceeds the gap we report against it. The twin has since also been run at scale on the Cellular Potts workload (Table~\ref{tab:twin-cpm}), and there the answer is more qualified. The barrier costs $1.9\times$ from one to four nodes and $2.8\times$ at eight, while pyABC's penalty over the same range grows from $2.4\times$ to $5.1\times$: the barrier is the largest single component we can identify, but it is not the whole gap, and the residual --- pyABC's probabilistic-rejection step, its classical population weighting, its own coordination --- grows with worker count. On the realistic simulator we therefore attribute the gain to synchronization only in part, and say so rather than generalizing the straggler result. The attribution now covers all three benchmarks on which we report systems gains, with the caveat that its strength differs across them: near-total under a straggler or heterogeneity, partial on Cellular Potts. Separately, the twin is a diagnostic rather than a robust implementation --- it intermittently deadlocks at $\ge192$ ranks (five hangs while collecting Table~\ref{tab:twin-cpm}), so replicates there needed retrying, though all twenty entries are complete runs. Separately, on a methodological note for reproducibility: the post-run retroactive estimator builds its denominator from $m\le S{+}1=21$ history-reconstructed proposals (with a defensive prior floor), not the full evaluated history; for a near-instantaneous simulator whose history reaches many millions of particles the pass is evaluated in bounded-memory chunks, which changes memory, not the estimate.

\section{Conclusion}\label{sec:conclusion}
We presented a generation-free, single-arrival-driven ABC algorithm that combines smooth-kernel ABC with streaming AMIS reweighting, reports an estimator that is a pure function of the evaluated history and is therefore recoverable by replay, and integrates into Propulate's barrier-free island model. The estimator it reports is consistent, and satisfies a central limit theorem with an explicit asymptotic variance under rate conditions we measure rather than assume --- finding that the configuration reported here does not meet them, so the central limit theorem covers damped-adaptation variants; both are proved directly rather than inherited, under conditions relating its denominator to the density its sample was drawn from, which we state precisely, decompose, and partly measure on production runs --- but do not establish for the configuration the experiments use. The reported posterior is validated empirically by simulation-based calibration --- well calibrated in low dimensions and under multimodality, and conservative rather than over-confident in higher dimensions, where we trace the residual miscalibration to how much of the evaluated history is reported (the efficient top-$k$ proposal would under-cover; the full history over-covers) rather than to the weighting. Against a synchronous pyABC baseline matched on the ABC kernel and bandwidth schedule, and scored on each method's own reported posterior against a reference posterior rather than on a point mass at the truth, the verdict splits by dimension --- the asynchronous method is roughly twice as accurate on the one-dimensional analytic target and roughly twice as inaccurate on the four-dimensional one, where its weights intermittently degenerate and accuracy tracks the effective sample size at a log--log correlation of $-0.80$ --- while the method delivers substantial HPC benefits on heterogeneous and straggler-prone workloads, including a realistic Cellular Potts simulation.

That comparison also turned up the result we would most want a reader to carry away, because it bounds the first one. The reported posterior's effective sample size is set by the archive size and not by the simulation budget: $237$--$303$ effective particles out of $1.1$--$1.3\times10^6$ evaluated, flat across a thirteen-fold growth of the history within a single run, with $\mathrm{ESS}/k$ between $1.9$ and $3.6$ across a controlled sweep of both. Reporting at the tightest bandwidth reached, while that bandwidth is chosen by ESS-retention bisection against the archive, leaves an effective support of a few multiples of $k$ no matter how many particles are evaluated --- and it does so exactly when the target is identified enough for the bandwidth to tighten, which is to say exactly when the posterior is worth having. Removing the barrier therefore buys evaluations, reliably and by one to two-and-a-half orders of magnitude on the workloads we test; converting those evaluations into posterior accuracy is a separate problem, and on this evidence it is the one that now limits the method. We think generation-free ABC is the right execution model for large-scale simulator-based inference on modern HPC systems, and that the next work on it belongs in the reporting rule rather than in the scheduler.

\subsection{Setting}\label{app:theory-setting}
We work with a filtration $(\mathcal{F}_i)$ for which, writing $\mathbb{E}_{i-1}[\cdot]=\mathbb{E}[\cdot\mid\mathcal{F}_{i-1}]$, the pair $\xi_i=(\theta_i,\rho_i)$ is $\mathcal{F}_i$-measurable and the two conditional laws below hold. Particles are indexed by \emph{proposal} time, not arrival time. This is what makes the martingale structure survive asynchronous execution: a worker forms its proposal from the history it has received at that moment, which is part of $\mathcal{F}_{i-1}$, so the conditional density $\tilde q_i$ of the emitted candidate is $\mathcal{F}_{i-1}$-measurable and
\begin{equation}
\theta_i\mid\mathcal{F}_{i-1}\sim \tilde q_i,
\qquad
\rho_i\mid\mathcal{F}_{i-1}\vee\sigma(\theta_i)\sim p(\cdot\mid\theta_i).
\label{eq:app-conditional-law}
\end{equation}
One further mismatch deserves naming, since it is between the theory and the software rather than inside either. The proofs index by \emph{proposal} time, whereas the implementation's post-hoc estimator consumes a rank's \emph{arrival}-ordered log and reconstructs the $q_{\tau_s}$ from prefixes of it. The two orderings agree for a sequential run and need not agree otherwise, so for an asynchronous run the object the code computes is the estimator of \eqref{eq:posterior-estimator} evaluated on a different, equally valid history; we do not prove that the theorems transfer between the two, and the discrepancy is one more contributor to $r$. Under sequential execution $\mathcal{F}_i=\sigma(\xi_1,\dots,\xi_i)$ serves, and \eqref{eq:app-conditional-law} holds by construction. Under \emph{asynchronous} execution it is a genuine assumption, and worth stating precisely what is being assumed. A discrepancy $\rho_i$ may still be in flight when candidate $i+1$ is proposed, so the $\sigma$-field of everything \emph{realized} by that moment does not contain $\xi_i$, and Lemma~\ref{lem:uslln} and the martingale arrays cannot use it. One must instead take a filtration large enough to contain the in-flight outcomes. Assumption~\ref{ass:filtration} asserts that such a filtration exists \emph{with $\tilde q_i$ still the conditional law of the candidate under it}. That is the substantive clause: a worker forms its proposal from the history it has received, so the proposal density is measurable with respect to the smaller field, but conditioning on the larger one need not leave the candidate's law unchanged when completion times steer which rank proposes next. When it does change, $\tilde q_i$ should be read as the enlarged-field conditional law rather than as the propagator's emitted density, and \eqref{eq:fidelity} is a statement about that object. This is the runtime-dependent censoring of \S\ref{sec:limitations}, item (iv), seen from the theory side, and we do not prove it away. Concurrency also affects the reconstruction of the $q_{\tau_s}$ from a single rank's arrival-ordered log, which enters only through $\|\bar q_n-\bar q_\infty\|_\infty$ in Assumption~\ref{ass:stab}.

No structural form is imposed on $\tilde q_i$: it is whatever the archive rule, the capped rejection, the underflow redraws and the bootstrap phase jointly produce, and it enters the proofs only through $\bar q^\star_n=\tfrac1n\sum_{i\le n}\tilde q_i$ of \eqref{eq:draw-mixture}, for which the reported denominator $\bar q_n$ of \eqref{eq:snapshot-denominator} is an $m$-component stand-in. Let $\mathcal{Q}$ be the set of positive functions of the form \eqref{eq:snapshot-denominator} whose components satisfy Assumption~\ref{ass:class}; nothing below requires an element of $\mathcal{Q}$ to integrate to one. Elements of $\mathcal{Q}$ are parameterized by $p_q=(\text{means},\text{component weights},\text{covariances},\alpha)$ ranging over a compact set $P_q\subset\mathbb{R}^{D}$, $D=m\,(kd+k+d(d+1)/2)+m$.

\begin{lemma}[bounded weights]\label{lem:bounded}
Under Assumptions~\ref{ass:class}--\ref{ass:defensive}, $0\le W_{i,n}\le1/\delta$ for all $i\le n$ and all $n$, surely --- whatever the sampler does.
\end{lemma}
\begin{proof}
All components of \eqref{eq:snapshot-denominator} are non-negative, so $\bar q_n\ge\delta\pi$ pointwise on $\Theta$. Hence $W_{i,n}=\pi(\theta_i)K_{\epsilon_n}(\rho_i)/\bar q_n(\theta_i)\le K_{\epsilon_n}(\rho_i)/\delta\le1/\delta$, the last step by $K_\epsilon\le1$ (Assumption~\ref{ass:kernel}). The prior cancels exactly, so the bound needs neither a density-ratio condition nor a moment condition.
\end{proof}

\begin{lemma}[uniform SLLN for bounded martingale differences]\label{lem:uslln}
Let $P\subset\mathbb{R}^{D}$ be compact and $\{\psi_p\}_{p\in P}$ measurable functions on a measurable space $\mathcal{X}$ with $\sup_{p}\|\psi_p\|_\infty\le B$ and $|\psi_p(x)-\psi_{p'}(x)|\le\Lambda\|p-p'\|$ for every $x\in\mathcal{X}$. Let $(\xi_i)$ be $\mathcal{X}$-valued with $\xi_i$ $\mathcal{F}_i$-measurable. Then
\[
\sup_{p\in P}\Bigl|\tfrac1n\sum_{i\le n}\bigl(\psi_p(\xi_i)-\mathbb{E}_{i-1}\psi_p(\xi_i)\bigr)\Bigr|\;\longrightarrow\;0
\qquad\text{a.s.}
\]
\end{lemma}
\begin{proof}
Fix $p$. The variables $D^p_i:=\psi_p(\xi_i)-\mathbb{E}_{i-1}\psi_p(\xi_i)$ form a martingale difference sequence with $|D^p_i|\le2B$, so by the Azuma--Hoeffding inequality $\mathbb{P}(|\sum_{i\le n}D^p_i|\ge nt)\le2\exp(-nt^2/8B^2)$ for every $t>0$. Fix $\eta>0$ and let $P_\eta$ be a finite $\eta$-net of $P$, of cardinality at most $C_P\eta^{-D}$. A union bound gives $\mathbb{P}(\max_{p\in P_\eta}|\sum_{i\le n}D^p_i|\ge nt)\le2C_P\eta^{-D}\exp(-nt^2/8B^2)$, which is summable in $n$ (the net is fixed, so its cardinality does not grow with $n$); by Borel--Cantelli, $\limsup_n\max_{p\in P_\eta}|\tfrac1n\sum_{i\le n}D^p_i|\le t$ a.s. For arbitrary $p\in P$ pick $p'\in P_\eta$ with $\|p-p'\|\le\eta$; then $|D^p_i-D^{p'}_i|\le2\Lambda\eta$ by the Lipschitz hypothesis (which also bounds the conditional expectations), so $\limsup_n\sup_{p\in P}|\tfrac1n\sum_{i\le n}D^p_i|\le t+2\Lambda\eta$ a.s. Intersecting the almost-sure events over the countable family $t=1/r$, $\eta=1/j$ with $r,j\in\mathbb{N}$, gives the claim.
\end{proof}

\begin{lemma}[conditional-mean identity]\label{lem:condmean}
Let $\phi:\Theta\to\mathbb{R}$ be bounded measurable, let $\ell\in\{1,2\}$ (an exponent, not the fidelity ratio $r$), and let $\epsilon\in[a,\epsilon_0]$ for some $a>0$ and the positive function $q\ge\delta\pi$ be either deterministic or $\mathcal{F}_{i-1}$-measurable. Then under \eqref{eq:app-conditional-law}, writing $L^{(1)}_\epsilon:=L_\epsilon$,
\[
\mathbb{E}_{i-1}\!\left[\frac{\phi(\theta_i)\,K_\epsilon(\rho_i)^{\ell}}{q(\theta_i)^{\ell}}\right]
=\int_\Theta \phi\,L^{(\ell)}_\epsilon\,\frac{\tilde q_i}{q^{\ell}}\,d\theta .
\]
\end{lemma}
\begin{proof}
Condition on $\mathcal{F}_{i-1}$, given which $\epsilon$ and $q$ are constants, and integrate first over $\rho_i\sim p(\cdot\mid\theta_i)$, which gives the factor $L^{(\ell)}_\epsilon(\theta_i)=\mathbb{E}[K_\epsilon(\rho)^\ell\mid\theta_i]$, then over $\theta_i\sim\tilde q_i$. The $\mathcal{F}_{i-1}$-measurable case is what Steps~2 and 3 of the proof of Theorem~\ref{thm:clt} need; an $\mathcal{F}_\infty$-measurable $q$ --- such as the limit $\bar q_\infty$ --- would \emph{not} be admissible here, which is why that proof centres on predictable proxies rather than on the limit. Fubini applies because the integrand is bounded by $\|\phi\|_\infty/(\delta\pi_-)^{\ell}$. The case $\ell=1$, $\phi=\pi h$ is the one used for first moments; $\ell=2$, $\phi=\pi^2h^2$ gives the conditional second moments of Step~3(b) and Corollary~\ref{cor:ci}.
\end{proof}

\paragraph{The class used below.} Take $\mathcal{X}=\Theta\times[0,\infty)$, $\xi_i=(\theta_i,\rho_i)$, and, for a fixed bounded measurable $h$,
\begin{equation}
\psi_{(\epsilon,p_q)}(\theta,\rho):=\frac{\pi(\theta)\,h(\theta)\,K_\epsilon(\rho)}{q_{p_q}(\theta)},
\qquad
(\epsilon,p_q)\in P_a:=[a,\epsilon_0]\times P_q,\qquad a\in(0,\epsilon_0] .
\label{eq:app-class}
\end{equation}
Each $P_a$ is compact \emph{and deterministic}, which matters: Lemma~\ref{lem:uslln} cannot be applied over a parameter set that depends on the run, and $[\epsilon_\infty,\epsilon_0]$ would be such a set when $\epsilon_\infty$ is random. We therefore apply the lemma on $P_{1/j}$ for every $j\in\mathbb{N}$ with $1/j\le\epsilon_0$ and intersect the countably many almost-sure events; since $\epsilon_n\downarrow\epsilon_\infty>0$ a.s., each path has $\epsilon_n\in[1/j,\epsilon_0]$ for all $n$ once $1/j\le\epsilon_\infty$, so the resulting uniform bound covers $(\epsilon_n,\bar q_n)$ on that path. No deterministic bandwidth floor is required. On $P_a$, $\sup\|\psi\|_\infty\le\|h\|_\infty/\delta$ by the computation of Lemma~\ref{lem:bounded}. For the Lipschitz hypothesis: each truncated component density $\varphi(\theta;\mu_j,\Sigma)/Z(\mu_j,\Sigma)$ has parameter-derivatives bounded uniformly over $\theta\in\Theta$, because $\Theta$ is compact, $\sigma_-^2I\preceq\Sigma\preceq\sigma_+^2I$ bounds $\varphi$ and its derivatives, and the truncation mass is bounded below, $Z(\mu_j,\Sigma)\ge c_0>0$, uniformly for $\mu_j\in\Theta$. Here it matters that the implementation's constant is the \emph{product of per-axis} in-box masses, not the exact joint mass: each factor is $\Phi((\mathrm{hi}_d-\mu_{jd})/\sigma_d)-\Phi((\mathrm{lo}_d-\mu_{jd})/\sigma_d)$, and since $\mu_{jd}$ lies in $[\mathrm{lo}_d,\mathrm{hi}_d]$ that interval contains a half-width $w_d/2$ on one side of $\mu_{jd}$, giving a factor at least $\Phi(w_d/2\sigma_+)-\tfrac12>0$; the product over coordinates, $\prod_{d}\bigl(\Phi(w_d/2\sigma_+)-\tfrac12\bigr)$, is therefore bounded below by a constant depending only on the box and $\sigma_+$, and is smooth in $(\mu_j,\Sigma)$ with bounded derivatives there. This is where Assumption~\ref{ass:class}'s box hypothesis is used; compactness of $\Theta$ alone would not give it. Hence every $q\in\mathcal{Q}$ --- a set of positive \emph{functions}, not necessarily normalized (Assumption~\ref{ass:class}) --- obeys $\delta\pi_-\le q\le q_+<\infty$ and $q\mapsto q(\theta)$ is Lipschitz in $p_q$ uniformly in $\theta$; so is $1/q$, since $q$ is bounded away from $0$. With $K_\epsilon$ Lipschitz in $\epsilon$ (Assumption~\ref{ass:kernel}) and $\pi h$ bounded, \eqref{eq:app-class} satisfies the hypotheses of Lemma~\ref{lem:uslln}. Note that on $\{1/j\le\epsilon_\infty\}$ we have $(\epsilon_n,\bar q_n)\in P_{1/j}$ for every $n$, and that $\bar q_\infty\ge\delta\pi$ by taking uniform limits.

\subsection{Proof of Theorem~\ref{thm:consistency}}\label{app:theory-consistency}
Work on the event $\{1/j\le\epsilon_\infty\}$ for a fixed $j$, so that $\widehat p_n:=(\epsilon_n,\bar q_n)\in P_{1/j}$ for every $n$; the conclusion then follows on the union over $j$, which has probability one. Fix a bounded measurable $h$ and define, for each \emph{fixed} $p\in P_{1/j}$, $\Gamma_i(p):=\mathbb{E}_{i-1}[\psi_p(\xi_i)]$ and $\bar\Gamma_n(p):=\tfrac1n\sum_{i\le n}\Gamma_i(p)$. On the almost-sure event of Lemma~\ref{lem:uslln} for $P_{1/j}$ the bound holds simultaneously for all $p\in P_{1/j}$, so the \emph{function} $\bar\Gamma_n$ may be evaluated at the random $\widehat p_n$:
\begin{equation}
\frac1n\sum_{i\le n}W_{i,n}h(\theta_i)
=\bar\Gamma_n(\widehat p_n)+o(1)
\overset{\text{Lem.\ \ref{lem:condmean}}}{=}\int_\Theta \pi\,h\,L_{\epsilon_n}\,\frac{\bar q^\star_n}{\bar q_n}\,d\theta+o(1)
\quad\text{a.s.},
\label{eq:app-consistency-main}
\end{equation}
using $\tfrac1n\sum_{i\le n}\tilde q_i=\bar q^\star_n$. The middle term is the function $\bar\Gamma_n(\cdot)$ evaluated at $\widehat p_n$ and must not be read as $\mathbb{E}_{i-1}[\psi_{\widehat p_n}(\xi_i)]$: $\widehat p_n$ depends on draws after $i$, is not $\mathcal{F}_{i-1}$-measurable, and that conditional expectation is not what Lemma~\ref{lem:uslln} controls. Split the integral against $\int\pi hL_{\epsilon_\infty}$:
\[
\Bigl|\int \pi h L_{\epsilon_n}\tfrac{\bar q^\star_n}{\bar q_n}-\int\pi hL_{\epsilon_\infty}r\Bigr|
\le \|h\|_\infty\,\pi_+|\Theta|\,\Bigl[\,R\,L_K(1/j)\,|\epsilon_n-\epsilon_\infty|
+\Bigl\|\tfrac{\bar q^\star_n}{\bar q_n}-r\Bigr\|_\infty\Bigr],
\]
on the event $\{1/j\le\epsilon_\infty\}$ of the localization above, using $|L_{\epsilon_n}-L_{\epsilon_\infty}|\le L_K(1/j)|\epsilon_n-\epsilon_\infty|$ (Assumption~\ref{ass:kernel} and Jensen; every $\epsilon_n$ and $\epsilon_\infty$ lies in $[1/j,\epsilon_0]$ there) and $L_{\epsilon_\infty}\le1$, and where $R<\infty$ bounds $\bar q^\star_n/\bar q_n$ for all large $n$ --- not from any structural bound on $\tilde q_i$, of which we assume none, but because \eqref{eq:fidelity} sends that ratio uniformly to $r\le r_+$. Both bracketed terms vanish, the first because $\epsilon_n\to\epsilon_\infty$ and the second by \eqref{eq:fidelity}. Hence $\tfrac1n\sum_iW_{i,n}h(\theta_i)\to\int\pi hL_{\epsilon_\infty}r$ a.s. Applying this with $h=f$ and with $h\equiv1$ and dividing --- legitimate because $Z_r=\int\pi L_{\epsilon_\infty}r\ge r_-Z_{\epsilon_\infty}>0$ --- gives $\widehat\pi_n(f)\to\pi^r_{\epsilon_\infty}(f)$ a.s. Corollary~\ref{cor:exact} is the case $r\equiv1$.\hfill$\square$

\begin{remark}\label{rem:weaker-c4}
Consistency used Assumption~\ref{ass:stab}(i) only through \eqref{eq:fidelity} and $\epsilon_n\to\epsilon_\infty$, and nothing at all about the form of $\tilde q_i$. This is what makes the theorem a statement about the algorithm as implemented rather than about an idealized variant of it: any sampler whose draw mixture the reported denominator tracks, in the ratio sense of \eqref{eq:fidelity}, is covered --- the archive rule, the capped rejection and the underflow redraws included. It is also the content that earlier drafts' condition on convergence to the \emph{idealized} cumulative mixture was missing: that object is not what the argument needs, and on its own does not deliver it.
\end{remark}

\subsection{Proof of Theorem~\ref{thm:clt} and Corollary~\ref{cor:ci}}\label{app:theory-clt}
Let $g:=f-\pi^r_{\epsilon_\infty}(f)$, a fixed bounded function --- Assumption~\ref{ass:stab}(ii) makes $\epsilon_\infty$ and $r$, hence $\pi^r_{\epsilon_\infty}(f)$, deterministic --- satisfying $\int\pi gL_{\epsilon_\infty}r=0$. Since $\sum_iW_{i,n}g(\theta_i)=(\sum_iW_{i,n})(\widehat\pi_n(f)-\pi^r_{\epsilon_\infty}(f))$ identically and $\tfrac1n\sum_iW_{i,n}\to Z_r>0$ a.s.\ (Theorem~\ref{thm:consistency} with $h\equiv1$), by Slutsky's theorem --- which preserves stable convergence --- it suffices to prove
\begin{equation}
S_n:=\frac{1}{\sqrt n}\sum_{i\le n}\frac{\pi(\theta_i)g(\theta_i)K_{\epsilon_n}(\rho_i)}{\bar q_n(\theta_i)}
\;\longrightarrow\;\mathcal{N}(0,v^2)\ \text{stably},
\qquad
v^2:=\int_\Theta\pi^2g^2L^{(2)}_{\epsilon_\infty}\frac{\tilde q_\infty}{\bar q^2_\infty}\,d\theta,
\label{eq:app-clt-target}
\end{equation}
after which $\sigma^2_f=v^2/Z^2_r$, which is \eqref{eq:clt-variance}.

\emph{Step 1 (replacing the parameters by predictable proxies).} $\bar q_\infty$ and $\epsilon_\infty$ are in general only $\mathcal{F}_\infty$-measurable, so an array built from them is \emph{not} adapted and Lemma~\ref{lem:condmean} does not apply to it. We therefore centre on the predictable pair instead: let $\bar q_{i-1}\in\mathcal{Q}$ be the denominator \eqref{eq:snapshot-denominator} formed from the first $i-1$ draws and $\epsilon_{i-1}$ the bandwidth in force at the $i$-th proposal, both $\mathcal{F}_{i-1}$-measurable, and put
\[
V_i:=\frac{\pi(\theta_i)\,g(\theta_i)\,K_{\epsilon_{i-1}}(\rho_i)}{\bar q_{i-1}(\theta_i)} .
\]
Since $\bar q_n,\bar q_{i-1}\ge\delta\pi_-$ and $K\le1$, and $|\epsilon_n-\epsilon_{i-1}|\le|\epsilon_n-\epsilon_\infty|+|\epsilon_{i-1}-\epsilon_\infty|$ (likewise for $\bar q$),
\[
\Bigl|S_n-\tfrac{1}{\sqrt n}\sum_{i\le n}V_i\Bigr|
\le C\sqrt n\bigl(|\epsilon_n-\epsilon_\infty|+\|\bar q_n-\bar q_\infty\|_\infty\bigr)
+\frac{C}{\sqrt n}\sum_{i\le n}\bigl(|\epsilon_{i-1}-\epsilon_\infty|+\|\bar q_{i-1}-\bar q_\infty\|_\infty\bigr)
\]
with $C=\|g\|_\infty\pi_+\max\{L_K(\epsilon_\infty)/(\delta\pi_-),(\delta\pi_-)^{-2}\}$ --- a single constant here, since Assumption~\ref{ass:stab}(ii) makes $\epsilon_\infty$ deterministic, so the central limit theorem needs no localization, and both brackets vanish by \eqref{eq:rate}. This step is what the rates buy; the remainder of the proof never touches $\bar q_n$ or $\epsilon_n$ again.

\emph{Step 2 (drift).} Lemma~\ref{lem:condmean} with $\ell=1$, $\phi=\pi g$ applies to $V_i$ because $(\epsilon_{i-1},\bar q_{i-1})$ is $\mathcal{F}_{i-1}$-measurable and $g$ is a fixed function under the central-limit clause of Assumption~\ref{ass:stab}; with $\int\pi gL_{\epsilon_\infty}r=0$,
\[
c_i:=\mathbb{E}_{i-1}V_i=\int\pi g\Bigl(L_{\epsilon_{i-1}}\frac{\tilde q_i}{\bar q_{i-1}}-L_{\epsilon_\infty}r\Bigr)d\theta,
\]
\[
|c_i|\le C'\bigl(|\epsilon_{i-1}-\epsilon_\infty|+\|\tilde q_i-\tilde q_\infty\|_\infty+\|\bar q_{i-1}-\bar q_\infty\|_\infty\bigr),
\]
$C'$ depending only on $\|g\|_\infty,\pi_\pm,\delta,|\Theta|,L_K(\epsilon_\infty)$. The second half of \eqref{eq:rate} is exactly the statement that $n^{-1/2}\sum_{i\le n}$ of that bound tends to zero, so $n^{-1/2}\sum_{i\le n}c_i\to0$. (Under the sufficient condition that each of the three terms is $O(i^{-1/2-a})$ this holds in each of the cases $a<1/2$, $a=1/2$, $a>1/2$, where the partial sums are $O(n^{1/2-a})$, $O(\log n)$ and $O(1)$ respectively.)

\emph{Step 3 (martingale CLT).} Let $X_{n,i}:=n^{-1/2}(V_i-c_i)$, a martingale difference array with respect to the filtration $(\mathcal{F}_i)$, which does not depend on $n$ and is therefore nested. Two conditions:
\emph{(a) Conditional Lindeberg.} $|X_{n,i}|\le2\|g\|_\infty\pi_+/(\delta\pi_-\sqrt n)$ surely, so for every $\eta>0$ the truncated sum $\sum_i\mathbb{E}_{i-1}[X^2_{n,i}\mathbf{1}\{|X_{n,i}|>\eta\}]$ is exactly zero for all $n$ large. The defensive floor makes this step trivial where an unbounded-weight scheme would need a uniform-integrability argument.
\emph{(b) Conditional variance.} Lemma~\ref{lem:condmean} with $\ell=2$, $\phi=\pi^2g^2$ gives
\[
\mathbb{E}_{i-1}V_i^2=\int\frac{\pi^2g^2L^{(2)}_{\epsilon_{i-1}}}{\bar q^2_{i-1}}\,\tilde q_i\,d\theta
=v^2+R_i,
\]
\[
v^2=\int\pi^2g^2L^{(2)}_{\epsilon_\infty}\frac{\tilde q_\infty}{\bar q^2_\infty}\,d\theta,
\qquad
|R_i|\le C''\bigl(|\epsilon_{i-1}-\epsilon_\infty|+\|\tilde q_i-\tilde q_\infty\|_\infty+\|\bar q_{i-1}-\bar q_\infty\|_\infty\bigr),
\]
where $C''$ carries $2L_K(\epsilon_\infty)$ for the squared kernel, since $|K^2_\epsilon-K^2_{\epsilon'}|=|K_\epsilon-K_{\epsilon'}|(K_\epsilon+K_{\epsilon'})\le2L_K(\epsilon_\infty)|\epsilon-\epsilon'|$. The bound on $|R_i|$ has vanishing Ces\`aro average, a fortiori from \eqref{eq:rate}, so $\tfrac1n\sum_{i\le n}\mathbb{E}_{i-1}V^2_i\to v^2$ a.s.; with $\tfrac1n\sum_ic^2_i\to0$ from Step 2,
$\sum_i\mathbb{E}_{i-1}X^2_{n,i}=\tfrac1n\sum_i(\mathbb{E}_{i-1}V^2_i-c^2_i)\to v^2$ a.s. Note that $v^2$ is permitted to be random here --- an $\mathcal{F}_\infty$-measurable limit of the conditional variances is exactly the setting the martingale CLT below covers, and it is only inside $\mathbb{E}_{i-1}$ that predictability was needed.
The martingale central limit theorem for arrays with nested $\sigma$-fields, in its conditional-variance form \cite[Cor.~3.1, pp.~58--59]{hall1980martingale}, then gives $\sum_iX_{n,i}\to\mathcal{N}(0,v^2)$ stably; the convergence is stable rather than merely distributional precisely because $v^2$ is $\mathcal{F}_\infty$-measurable and possibly random. Combining Steps 1--3 gives \eqref{eq:app-clt-target}.\hfill$\square$

\begin{proof}[Proof of Corollary~\ref{cor:ci}]
Write $\widehat\sigma^2_n=[\tfrac1n\sum_iW^2_{i,n}(f(\theta_i)-\widehat\pi_n(f))^2]\,/\,[\tfrac1n\sum_iW_{i,n}]^2$. In the numerator, $(f-\widehat\pi_n(f))^2=g^2+2g(\pi^r_{\epsilon_\infty}(f)-\widehat\pi_n(f))+(\pi^r_{\epsilon_\infty}(f)-\widehat\pi_n(f))^2$, and $W_{i,n}\le1/\delta$ with $\widehat\pi_n(f)\to\pi^r_{\epsilon_\infty}(f)$ a.s.\ (Theorem~\ref{thm:consistency}), so the cross terms vanish and it suffices to treat $\tfrac1n\sum_iW^2_{i,n}g(\theta_i)^2$. Apply Lemma~\ref{lem:uslln} on $P_{a}$ with $a=\epsilon_\infty$ --- deterministic here, so no localization is needed --- to the class $\psi_{(\epsilon,p_q)}(\theta,\rho)=\pi(\theta)^2g(\theta)^2K_\epsilon(\rho)^2/q_{p_q}(\theta)^2$, which is bounded by $\|g\|^2_\infty/\delta^2$ and Lipschitz in $(\epsilon,p_q)$ by the argument of \eqref{eq:app-class} with $2L_K(\epsilon_\infty)$ in place of $L_K$; the substitution of $\widehat p_n$ used in \eqref{eq:app-consistency-main}, followed by Lemma~\ref{lem:condmean} with $\ell=2$, $\phi=\pi^2g^2$ (a fixed function under Assumption~\ref{ass:stab}(ii)), gives
$\tfrac1n\sum_iW^2_{i,n}g^2(\theta_i)=\int\pi^2g^2L^{(2)}_{\epsilon_n}\,\bigl(\bar q^\star_n/\bar q_n\bigr)\,\bar q_n^{-1}+o(1)$,
and $\epsilon_n\to\epsilon_\infty$, $\|\bar q^\star_n/\bar q_n-r\|_\infty\to0$ and $\|\bar q_n-\bar q_\infty\|_\infty\to0$ (the last from \eqref{eq:rate}) send this to $\int\pi^2g^2L^{(2)}_{\epsilon_\infty}r/\bar q_\infty=\int\pi^2g^2L^{(2)}_{\epsilon_\infty}\tilde q_\infty/\bar q^2_\infty=v^2$ a.s., using $r=\tilde q_\infty/\bar q_\infty$. The denominator tends to $Z^2_r$, so $\widehat\sigma^2_n\to v^2/Z^2_r=\sigma^2_f$ a.s. Because the convergence in Theorem~\ref{thm:clt} is stable, the pair $(\sqrt n(\widehat\pi_n(f)-\pi^r_{\epsilon_\infty}(f)),\widehat\sigma_n)$ converges jointly to $(\sigma_f\mathcal{Z},\sigma_f)$ --- stable convergence is exactly joint convergence with every $\mathcal{F}_\infty$-measurable variable, and $\sigma_f$ is one --- so with $\sigma_f>0$ a.s.\ the continuous mapping theorem gives $\mathcal{Z}\sim\mathcal{N}(0,1)$ as the limit of the ratio. Marginal convergence to a mixed normal alone would not suffice.
\end{proof}

\begin{remark}[growing snapshot count]\label{rem:growing-m}
Nothing above requires $m$ to be fixed except the compactness and fixed dimension $D$ of $P_q$ --- and the fixed floor $\delta$, which the implementation ties to $m$ as $0.5/(m{+}1)$ and which must be decoupled before $m$ is allowed to grow, since Lemma~\ref{lem:bounded}'s bound is $1/\delta$. With $\delta$ fixed and $m=m_n\to\infty$, run the proof of Lemma~\ref{lem:uslln} with an $n$-dependent net: the union bound costs $D_n\log(1/\eta_n)$ with $D_n=\Theta(m_n)$, and the Lipschitz constant over the mixture weights grows at most polynomially in $m_n$, so choosing $\eta_n\to0$ fast enough leaves the Borel--Cantelli step summable for any $m_n=o(n/\log n)$. In that regime the snapshot clause of Assumption~\ref{ass:stab} follows from $V_n/m_n\to0$, with $V_n=\sum_{i<n}\|q_{i+1}-q_i\|_\infty$ the total variation of the proposal path, rather than having to be assumed: the draw-proportional $m_n$-point mixture is then a converging quadrature of the empirical mixture. The retroactive pass costs $\mathcal{O}(n\,m_n\,k)$.
\end{remark}

\subsection{Proof of Proposition~\ref{prop:tilt}}\label{app:theory-tilt}
Write $\zeta_0:=\pi_{\epsilon_\infty}(|r-1|)$. Under the hypothesis, $\zeta_0\le\eta_A+\pi_{\epsilon_\infty}(A^c)=\zeta$. For bounded $f$, since $\pi^r_{\epsilon_\infty}(f)=\pi_{\epsilon_\infty}(fr)/\pi_{\epsilon_\infty}(r)$,
\[
\pi^r_{\epsilon_\infty}(f)-\pi_{\epsilon_\infty}(f)
=\frac{\pi_{\epsilon_\infty}\!\left(f(r-1)\right)-\pi_{\epsilon_\infty}(f)\,\pi_{\epsilon_\infty}(r-1)}{\pi_{\epsilon_\infty}(r)},
\]
whose modulus is at most $2\|f\|_\infty\zeta_0/(1-\zeta_0)$ because $\pi_{\epsilon_\infty}(r)\ge1-\zeta_0$. Taking the supremum over $\|f\|_\infty\le1$ and halving bounds the total variation by $\zeta_0/(1-\zeta_0)$; as $x\mapsto x/(1-x)$ increases on $[0,1)$, this is at most $\zeta/(1-\zeta)$ whenever $\zeta<1$, which is \eqref{eq:tilt-bound}. For $\zeta\ge1$ the bound degenerates and only the trivial $\|\cdot\|_{\mathrm{TV}}\le1$ survives.

The closed form quoted in \S\ref{sec:theory-r}(a) for the prior floor's contribution follows by evaluating \eqref{eq:fidelity} for that contribution alone: with $u=q_\infty/\pi$ and a limiting bootstrap share $\nu_\infty<\delta$,
\[
r_{\mathrm{floor}}-1=\frac{\nu_\infty+(1-\nu_\infty)u}{\delta+(1-\delta)u}-1=\frac{(\delta-\nu_\infty)(u-1)}{\delta+(1-\delta)u},
\]
so on $\{u\ge c\}$, splitting at $u=1$ and bounding each branch, $|r_{\mathrm{floor}}-1|\le(\delta-\nu_\infty)\max\{(1-\delta)^{-1},(\delta+(1-\delta)c)^{-1}\}$, which is the $\eta_A$ of Proposition~\ref{prop:tilt} for $A=\{u\ge c\}$. It is identically $1$ when $\nu_\infty\ge\delta$, and $\to1$ as $\delta\to0$.\hfill$\square$

\section{Implementation Details}\label{app:implementation}

\paragraph{Tolerance schedulers.} Every scheduler is a pure function of the evaluated history and is \emph{monotone by construction}: the propagator clips any proposed bandwidth above the history-reconstructed running minimum $\epsilon_{\text{hist}}$ (\S\ref{sec:method-tolerance}), so the effective tolerance can only decrease. Let $\mu$ denote the extra-individual margin (default $\mu=k$); it is unrelated to the snapshot count $m$ of \S\ref{sec:theory}. The three base rules act on the individuals \emph{within} the current bandwidth, i.e.\ the hard-threshold set $\{i:\rho_i<\epsilon\}$. (This bandwidth-membership count is used \emph{only} to drive the tolerance schedule; it is distinct from the smooth-kernel weight $K_\epsilon(\rho_i)$ that defines the proposal mixture and the reported posterior, for which every evaluated particle contributes continuously. We avoid the word ``accepted'' here precisely because acceptance is probabilistic under a smooth kernel.):
\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{Quantile.} $\epsilon \leftarrow$ the lower-rank (non-interpolated) $p$-th percentile of the accepted losses, default $p=50$; applied only once at least $k+m$ individuals are accepted.
\item \textbf{Geometric decay.} $\epsilon \leftarrow \epsilon_0\,\gamma^{\,e}$ after each completed epoch $e$ of $k+\mu$ accepted individuals, default $\gamma=0.9$.
\item \textbf{Acceptance rate.} Over a sliding window of the most recent $k+\mu$ individuals, if the acceptance rate exceeds $r_{\text{hi}}$ (default $0.3$) tighten by a factor $0.9$, otherwise hold; the rule never expands $\epsilon$ (the low-rate guard $r_{\text{lo}}=0.1$ simply holds).
\end{itemize}
With a smooth (Gaussian or Epanechnikov) kernel the schedulers run in a \emph{kernel-aware} mode: instead of the hard $\rho<\epsilon$ count, $\epsilon$ is chosen by bisection so that the per-step kernel-weighted effective-sample-size retention ratio meets a target (default $0.95$), which keeps the bandwidth schedule comparable across kernel choices.

\paragraph{Proposal covariance.} The archive $A_n$ is the top-$k$ history members by smallest discrepancy. Component weights are formed in log-space as $\tilde W_j \propto w_j\,K_{\epsilon_n}(\rho_j)$ and normalised (a uniform fallback is used if all smooth weights underflow). The perturbation covariance is $\Sigma_n = s\,[\,\widehat{\mathrm{Cov}}_{\tilde W}(A_n) + \lambda_n I\,]$ with $s$ the \texttt{perturbation\_scale} and a two-part jitter $\lambda_n = 10^{-9}\,\mathrm{tr}\,\widehat{\mathrm{Cov}}_{\tilde W}(A_n)/d + 10^{-12}\,\bar b^2$, where $\bar b^2$ is the mean squared prior-box width; it is Cholesky-factored once per call. The first term is scale-aware, so a tightly converged archive (marginal standard deviation ${\sim}10^{-4}$) is not swamped by the floor as a fixed $10^{-6}$ would swamp it; the second is a run-independent absolute floor that keeps the matrix factorable for a degenerate archive and supplies the uniform lower spectral bound Assumption~\ref{ass:class} needs (Table~\ref{tab:assumption-status}). Each mixture component is truncated to the prior box, with its log normalising mass computed as the product of the per-axis in-box masses. That is exact for a diagonal covariance, in which case the proposal integrates to one on the support; for a correlated covariance it is an approximation, so the evaluated proposal is a positive function that need not integrate to one --- which is why \S\ref{sec:theory} states its denominator assumption for positive functions and books the discrepancy as factor~(c) of $r$ (\S\ref{sec:theory-r}).

\paragraph{Matched pyABC acceptor.} For the synchronous baseline, pyABC's \texttt{UniformAcceptor} is replaced, for smooth kernels, by a probabilistic-rejection acceptor that accepts a candidate of discrepancy $\rho$ with probability $K_{\epsilon}(\rho)$ (peak-normalised so $K_{\epsilon}(0)=1$), using the \emph{same} kernel $K_{\epsilon}$ as the asynchronous side; the hard kernel falls back to \texttt{UniformAcceptor}. The rejection step of each replicate draws from an independently seeded generator. This is the sense in which the kernel and acceptor are matched across methods (\S\ref{sec:implementation}).

\paragraph{Bounded-memory posterior estimator.} The retroactive AMIS estimator \eqref{eq:posterior-estimator} evaluates the snapshot denominator $\bar q_n$ --- a draw-proportional mixture of $m\le S{+}1=21$ history-reconstructed proposals with a defensive prior component (mass floored at $0.5/(m{+}1)$) --- at each of the $n$ evaluated particles, at total cost $\mathcal{O}(n\,m\,k)=\mathcal{O}(nk)$ with $m$ fixed. The snapshots are taken draw-proportionally from the reconstructed archive (indices $0,\text{step},2\,\text{step},\dots$ with $\text{step}=\max(1,\lfloor|\text{archive}|/S\rfloor)$), plus the most recent archive-phase proposal if it is not already among them --- hence $m\le S{+}1=21$ rather than $S$. For near-instantaneous simulators the history reaches $\mathcal{O}(10^7)$ particles, so the pass is accumulated in chunks of $2^{16}=65{,}536$ history points: peak memory is $\mathcal{O}(\text{chunk}\cdot m\cdot k)$ rather than $\mathcal{O}(n)$. Each particle's denominator is evaluated independently within a single chunk (there is no log-sum-exp reduction \emph{across} chunk boundaries), so chunking changes memory, not the value, and the result is bit-identical to the unchunked computation. Benchmarked directly (\texttt{scripts/diag\_retrospective\_cost.py}, artifact committed) at $k=100$, $m=20$ over $n$ from $2.5\times10^4$ to $10^6$, the pass costs $49.3$~s per $10^6$ particles on a single core and is linear in $n$ to within a $7.9\%$ maximum relative residual, so the $3\times10^7$-particle Gaussian history extrapolates to ${\approx}25$~minutes (and $10^7$ to ${\approx}8$~minutes). Peak working set grows by $0.27$\,GB over that $40{\times}$ range of $n$ --- i.e.\ it is flat in $n$ as the chunking predicts, the intermediate being one $2^{16}$-point chunk. The pass parallelizes trivially across evaluation points; it runs after the timed budget and is excluded from the throughput measurements by design. For a near-instantaneous simulator this is comparable to the timed budget itself --- non-trivial though bounded --- whereas for cost-bearing simulators, whose histories are orders of magnitude shorter, it is negligible against the simulation time.

\section{Additional Experimental Protocol}\label{app:protocol}

\paragraph{Effective simulating ranks.} A ``$W$-worker'' point allocates $W$ physical cores to both arms, but the two arms use them differently. Every rank of the asynchronous method evaluates, so it has $W$ simulating ranks. pyABC's MPI sampler is root-driven: rank $0$ distributes work to ranks $1,\dots,W{-}1$ and does not simulate, so the baseline has $W{-}1$. We report the allocated core count $W$ for both, which flatters the asynchronous arm by $1/W$ --- $2.1\%$ at $W{=}48$, $0.3\%$ at $W{=}384$. We do not correct for it because the correction shrinks monotonically with scale while the gap we report grows with scale, so it cannot account for any reported effect; but it should be read as part of the coordination cost the barrier-free design avoids rather than as a measurement we have netted out.

\paragraph{Seeding.} Each experiment derives its replicate seeds deterministically from a single base seed via \texttt{make\_seeds}: a \texttt{random.Random(base)} stream emits unique non-negative 31-bit integers, one per replicate, portable across platforms and independent of any global RNG state. Derived seeds (observed-data generation, the pyABC rejection step) use a structured BLAKE2b hash (\texttt{stable\_seed}) of their inputs, so identical inputs reproduce identical seeds across fresh and resumed (\texttt{--extend}) runs.

\paragraph{Crash recovery (kill-and-resume).} We test the crash-recoverability claim of \S\ref{sec:method-constraints} directly. On the Gaussian-mean benchmark ($4$ workers, a $1.2\times10^{5}$-simulation budget) we compare a clean uninterrupted run against a run that is hard-killed (\texttt{SIGKILL} of the entire worker process group) partway through inference and then relaunched with an identical command; recovery reloads the periodic population checkpoint and resumes from the next generation. The resumed run recovers the analytic posterior mean --- absolute error $7.6\times10^{-3}$, against $7.5\times10^{-4}$ for the clean run --- and completes the same total simulation budget with no duplicated records. It is \emph{not} a bit-identical replay: the two reported posterior means differ by $6.9\times10^{-3}$. Three things prevent the replay, and they are worth separating. Two are incidental --- the candidate generator is re-seeded from its (replicate, rank) key on restart rather than checkpointed, and MPI arrival order is not deterministic. The third is structural: the online AMIS snapshot buffer is rebuilt empty on restart, and since it enters the importance denominator of every subsequent candidate, and those weights form the archive's proposal mixture, the post-restart proposal sequence is drawn from a different (though equally valid) adaptation trajectory (\S\ref{sec:method-constraints}). Checkpointing the buffer would remove the third but not the first two. This is exactly the sense in which the method is ``reproducible up to MPI arrival order'' (\S\ref{sec:method-constraints}): recovery yields a \emph{valid} posterior trajectory over the actual evaluated history, not the identical one. In this instance the kill landed \emph{during} a checkpoint write and truncated the primary checkpoint; recovery fell back to the previous-state backup that the dumper writes before overwriting, so the resume succeeded automatically --- the reported estimator, being a pure function of the recovered history, is unaffected by which of the two checkpoints is used.

\paragraph{Cluster and wall-clock control.} Experiments run on JUWELS (Forschungszentrum J\"ulich) under ParaStation MPI, 48 cores per node, scheduled with SLURM. All wall-clock-budgeted methods stop at a fixed wall-clock budget enforced uniformly by a monotonic-clock deadline with first-rank-hit semantics: once any rank trips the deadline it serialises its current state into the records and returns, so a partial final generation is recorded rather than discarded (the simulation-limited studies marked $\dagger$ in Table~\ref{tab:configs} --- the SBC and kill-and-resume runs --- instead stop at a fixed per-trial simulation count). Posterior estimation and plotting run off the timed inference path, so they do not count against the budget.

\paragraph{Benchmark-specific notes.} Each benchmark fixes one observed dataset (generated from seed $42$) and infers parameters under uniform priors; the discrepancy is a distance between simulated and observed summary statistics. \emph{Gaussian mean:} $n_{\text{obs}}=100$ observations $\sim\mathcal{N}(0,1)$, prior $\mu\sim\mathcal{U}[-5,5]$, summary the sample mean, discrepancy $|\bar x_{\text{sim}}-\bar x_{\text{obs}}|$; the closed-form Gaussian posterior is the calibration and recovery reference. \emph{g-and-k:} the quantile-defined g-and-k distribution ($c=0.8$), $n_{\text{obs}}=1000$, true $(A,B,g,k)=(3,1,2,0.5)$, priors $A\sim\mathcal{U}[0,6]$, $B\sim\mathcal{U}[0.1,4]$, $g\sim\mathcal{U}[0,5]$, $k\sim\mathcal{U}[0,1]$; summary the seven octiles (the $0.125,\dots,0.875$ quantiles), discrepancy Euclidean. \emph{Lotka--Volterra:} a Gillespie stochastic simulation of the four-reaction predator--prey system from $(X_0,Y_0)=(50,100)$ over $T=30$, true rates $\theta=(0.5,0.025,0.025,0.5)$, priors $\theta_1,\theta_4\sim\mathcal{U}[0.05,2]$ and $\theta_2,\theta_3\sim\mathcal{U}[0.001,0.2]$; summary the six statistics $(\overline X,\operatorname{sd}X,\overline Y,\operatorname{sd}Y,\log(1{+}\operatorname{var}X/\overline X),\log(1{+}\operatorname{var}Y/\overline Y))$, discrepancy Euclidean. \emph{Cellular Potts:} the real \texttt{cellsInSilico} (NAStJA) simulator, inferring the cell division rate and motility on normalized $[0,1]$ priors that map to physical ranges $[6{\times}10^{-5},0.6]$ and $[0,10^4]$ (true normalized values $0.0499$ and $0.2$); the discrepancy is a whitened distance over morphological summary features of the simulated cell configuration (radial density and pair-correlation profiles, free-area and structure-factor profiles, a DBSCAN dispersion fraction, and log cell count and radius) against reference summaries.
